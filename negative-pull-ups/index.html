Negative pull-ups: how eccentric training builds your first pull-up

An anatomy lesson for the lowering half of the rep·8 min read

A negative pull-up is the lowering half of a pull-up done on its own: you get your chin over the bar, then lower yourself slowly. Your lats, elbow flexors and lower trapezius keep working while they lengthen, and a muscle can handle more load in that mode than when it shortens. That makes negatives a practical stepping stone to a first pull-up, as long as you keep the dose small enough to recover from.

What is a negative pull-up?

A negative pull-up skips the pulling and trains the lowering. You use a jump or a step to reach the top position, with your chin above the bar and your arms bent. Then you lower yourself under control until your arms are straight.

Muscles can work in three ways. In a concentric contraction the muscle shortens while it produces force, which is the pulling-up part of a pull-up. In an isometric contraction it holds its length, which is the top hold or the hang. In an eccentric contraction the muscle produces force while it gets longer. A negative is almost pure eccentric work, which is why coaches also call it an eccentric pull-up.

Which muscles work while you lower, and how?

The same muscles that raise you up also control the way down. They just do their job in reverse, braking the movement instead of driving it.

The latissimus dorsi is a broad, flat muscle that runs from the lower spine, the thoracolumbar fascia and the pelvis up to the upper arm bone. Its main job is to move the arm.[1] With your hands fixed on a bar it pulls your trunk up instead. During a negative it is at its longest at the bottom, so that is where it works hardest to stop you from dropping. Our lat anatomy guide goes deeper.

The biceps brachii has two heads at the shoulder and crosses the elbow to attach to the radius. It flexes the forearm and, when the elbow is bent, it is the strongest supinator of the forearm.[2] The brachialis lies underneath it and is described as a pure flexor of the elbow that works in every physiological position.[3] That detail matters for negatives, because with an overhand grip the brachialis does a large share of the elbow work.

The lower trapezius pulls the shoulder blade down and helps rotate it upward.[4] At the top of the rep your shoulder blades are low. As you descend they want to ride up toward your ears, and the lower trapezius resists that. Your grip muscles hold the bar isometrically the whole time.

Why can you lower more than you can lift?

A review of eccentric muscle work reports that greater forces are generated during eccentric contractions than during other contraction types at the same angular velocity. It also reports that eccentric actions need less motor unit activation and use less energy for the same force.[5] In practice this means there is often a gap between the weight you can lower and the weight you can lift. If you cannot pull yourself up yet, you can frequently still lower yourself, and that is the opening negatives use.

What does the research say about training this way? A meta-analysis of 20 randomised trials found that eccentric training at higher intensities increased total strength and eccentric strength more than concentric training. The authors linked this to the higher loads that eccentric contractions allow. They also noted that the strength gains were quite specific to the velocity and mode of contraction, and that how far they carry over to complex movements needs more study.[6] A later systematic review of 40 studies reached a similar picture: greater strength improvements from eccentric training, largely mode-specific, plus changes in muscle architecture and tendon tissue.[7]

One small randomised trial looked at pull-ups directly. Thirty advanced climbers trained twice a week for five weeks on a hangboard with eccentric, isometric or plyometric work, and every training group improved maximum strength compared with a control group.[8] The groups were small (eight in the eccentric group) and the participants were experienced, so the result tells you little about beginners. We found no trial that tested negatives for a first pull-up, so treat the dosing below as a cautious coaching framework and not as a tested protocol.

How do you do a negative pull-up?

  1. Set up the top position. Stand on a box or bench under the bar, or jump up, so your chin ends above the bar with your arms bent. Using a chin-up grip (palms toward you) makes the top easier to reach and hold.
  2. Hold for one second. Pull your shoulder blades down, squeeze the bar and make sure you are not hanging from your neck.
  3. Lower slowly. Let your elbows straighten over a count of three to five seconds at first. Keep the speed as even as you can, with no drop in the middle.
  4. Finish in an active hang. Stop with your arms straight and your shoulders still lightly engaged. Do not let them collapse up into your ears.
  5. Step down and reset. Use the box again instead of jumping up from a dead hang. Rest before the next rep.

Cues that help

  • Start the descent by moving your shoulder blades first, then your elbows.
  • Keep your ribs down and your legs slightly in front of you so your body stays in one line.

How many sets, reps and days per week?

There is no research-backed prescription for beginners, so the plan below is conservative on purpose. It follows the idea that you add time under tension before you add reps.

StageLowering timeSets x repsDays per week
Weeks 1-23 seconds3 x 22
Weeks 3-44-5 seconds3 x 32
Weeks 5-65-6 seconds3-4 x 32

Rest two to three minutes between sets and leave at least one rest day between negative sessions. Two days a week matches the frequency used in the climber trial.[8] Move to the next stage only when every rep of the current one looks identical. If a week left you sore or your elbows complained, repeat it before you progress.

Why do negatives make you sore, and what about your tendons?

Unaccustomed eccentric exercise is known to cause muscle damage and delayed-onset muscle soreness. The soreness usually appears 12 to 24 hours after the session and peaks between 24 and 72 hours.[5] The most useful protective strategy in the same review is repeating sessions with submaximal eccentric work and raising the load gradually. After a first bout the muscle adapts, and the soreness from the next bout is typically smaller. That is the reason to begin with 3 x 2 even if you feel you could do more.

Tendons adapt too. The systematic review on chronic adaptations reports tendon changes that appear related to the amount of strain placed on them.[7] Negatives load the elbow flexor tendons and the shoulder at long muscle lengths, so a sharp, pinching or lingering pain at the elbow or shoulder is a reason to cut the volume and look at the cause. Our elbow tendon pain guide covers what to check.

What mistakes slow you down?

  • Dropping instead of lowering. A fast fall in the middle gives up the controlled loading that makes negatives useful and jolts the elbows.
  • Skipping the top position. If your chin does not start above the bar, you train only the lower part of the range.
  • Doing too much too early. Ten negatives in one session is a quick way to a week of sore arms and a stalled plan.
  • Shrugging. If your shoulders ride up in the first second, the lower trapezius has stopped doing its job.
  • Using negatives alone. Because strength gains from eccentric work are quite specific,[6] add other pulling work as well. See the list below.

How do negatives fit into getting your first pull-up?

Negatives train the lowering half and make you comfortable under load at the top and in the stretched hang. They do not train the first few centimetres of a concentric pull from a dead hang, and the research on specificity suggests you should not expect them to do it for you.[6] A first pull-up usually comes from combining several tools.

  • Negatives, two days a week, for the lowering strength described above.
  • Dead hangs and active hangs for grip and shoulder control. Our dead hang guide shows how to progress them.
  • Band-assisted pull-ups or rows for concentric practice on the way up. See band-assisted pull-ups.
  • A test attempt every two weeks. Try one full rep fresh, before your negatives, and note how far you get.

Frequently asked questions

Do negative pull-ups really help you get your first pull-up?

They are a reasonable part of a plan. Eccentric training builds strength, but the gains are fairly specific to lowering, so pair negatives with hangs and assisted pulling. We found no trial that tested negatives for a first pull-up.

How slow should a negative pull-up be?

Start with about three seconds and work toward five or six, with an even speed from top to bottom. The exact count is a coaching convention. What matters is that you control the whole descent.

How many negative pull-ups should I do per session?

A conservative start is three sets of two reps, then three sets of three. Stop the set as soon as the descent speeds up. More reps are not better if form falls apart.

Why am I so sore after negatives?

Eccentric work on an untrained muscle commonly causes delayed-onset muscle soreness, which peaks about 24 to 72 hours after the session. It usually fades with repeated, gradually increased sessions. Joint or tendon pain that lingers is different and worth checking.

Can I do negatives every day?

It is better not to. Eccentric work damages muscle fibres and they need time to recover. Two sessions a week with a rest day between them is a safer starting point.
