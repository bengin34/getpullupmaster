Scapular muscles and pull-ups: an anatomy lesson for trainees

Trapezius, rhomboids, serratus anterior, levator scapulae·9 min read

The scapular muscles are the trapezius, rhomboids, serratus anterior and levator scapulae. They move and anchor your shoulder blade, and in a pull-up they do the first job: pulling the shoulder blade down and in before your elbows bend. If you learn to control them, your pull-up gets stronger and your shoulders and neck take less strain.

Which muscles move the shoulder blade?

Your shoulder blade (scapula) is a flat bone that floats on the back of the rib cage. It has no real bony joint with the trunk; only the collarbone links it to the sternum. Muscles hold it in place and move it. Four of them matter most for pull-ups.

The scapula moves in three pairs. Elevation and depression: shrug your shoulders up toward your ears, then push them down away from your ears. Protraction and retraction: reach forward so the shoulder blades slide apart, then pull them toward your spine. Upward and downward rotation: raise your arm overhead and the lower tip of the blade swings out to the side; bring the arm down and it swings back in.

To feel them, stand with your back to a wall. Place one hand on the opposite shoulder blade. Shrug, depress, reach and squeeze while you feel the bone slide. Then raise the arm overhead and notice the lower angle moving outward: that is upward rotation.

What is scapulohumeral rhythm, and why does a pull-up start at the shoulder blade?

Your upper arm bone (humerus) and your shoulder blade move together. When you lift your arm overhead, roughly two thirds of the motion comes from the ball-and-socket joint and one third from the scapula rotating upward on the rib cage. The clinical name for this is scapulohumeral rhythm. Without the scapular share, the arm cannot reach fully overhead and the top of the humerus runs into the roof of the shoulder.

A pull-up is that overhead motion in reverse, under load. In a dead hang, your scapulae are upwardly rotated and elevated. The first inch of the pull is not an elbow bend. It is the scapula depressing and rotating downward: the lower trapezius pulls the blade down, the rhomboids and the lats pull it in and down. Only after that do the elbows start to flex. If you skip this step, you hang off your ligaments and try to start the rep with your biceps.

This is the difference between a passive hang and an active hang. In a passive hang you let go: shoulders up by your ears, scapulae elevated, the joint capsule carrying the load. In an active hang you pull the shoulder blades down and slightly back while your elbows stay straight. Your neck gets longer, your chest lifts a little, and you feel the muscles under your armpits and between your shoulder blades switch on. Both hangs have uses, which we cover in dead hang benefits, but every pull-up rep should start from the active one.

What does the shoulder blade do in each phase of a pull-up?

  • Dead hang. Scapulae elevated and upwardly rotated. Serratus anterior and upper trapezius hold the blade in that rotated position. This is your starting point, not where you rest for long.
  • Initiation. Lower trapezius depresses the scapula. Rhomboids and lats begin downward rotation. Your body rises a few centimeters with straight arms. This is the scapular pull-up, and it is the part most beginners never learn.
  • Mid-pull. The elbows flex. The lats do most of the lifting. The scapula keeps rotating downward and starts to retract. Serratus anterior still works to keep the blade flat against the ribs as the arm angle changes.
  • Top. Chin over the bar, scapulae depressed and retracted. Middle trapezius and rhomboids hold the squeeze. The shoulders stay away from the ears.
  • Lowering. Everything runs in reverse under control. The scapula protracts and rotates upward as the arms straighten. Finish in an active hang.

The common fault is the shrug. When the lower trapezius is weak or asleep, the body finds another way up: the upper trapezius and levator scapulae lift the shoulder blade toward the ear and the head pokes forward. You still get over the bar, but the load moves from the big back muscles to the neck. It is to start every rep from depression and to stop the set when you can no longer keep the shoulders down. More form errors are covered in pull-up mistakes.

What does the research say about scapular muscles in pull-ups?

There is little research on the scapula during pull-ups specifically, so treat the following as pointers, not rules. In a Mayo Clinic EMG study of 25 adults, average lower trapezius activity during pull-ups and chin-ups was 45–56% of maximal voluntary contraction, compared with 117–130% for the latissimus dorsi. The lower trapezius was significantly more active in the overhand pull-up than in the chin-up. The authors also noted a sequence: the reps appeared to be started by the lower trapezius and pectoralis major and finished by the biceps and lats[6].

A second study in 19 strength-trained men compared pull-up grips. Over a full rep, the overhand (pronated) grip produced roughly 60% of maximal activation in the middle trapezius at peak, versus 37% with a neutral grip, with average activation of 48% against 27%. Overall activation across the other muscles was similar between grips[7]. If your goal is to load the muscles between your shoulder blades, an overhand grip appears to do more of that than a neutral one. For how grip changes the rest of the picture, see chin-up vs pull-up.

The shoulder-health side comes from the clinical literature on scapular dyskinesis, meaning altered position or motion of the scapula. The 2013 consensus statement led by Kibler concluded that dyskinesis is present in a high percentage of shoulder injuries, that impingement symptoms are particularly affected by it, and that programmes to restore scapular position and motion can be effective as part of broader shoulder rehabilitation. The same statement is careful to say that the exact causal role of dyskinesis is not clearly defined and that it is best viewed as a potential impairment, not a diagnosis[5]. For a trainee, the practical reading is simple: a scapula that depresses, rotates and retracts well gives your shoulder more room to work, and training that control is cheap insurance.

How do you train scapular control? The scapular pull-up and other drills

The scapular pull-up (scap pull) is the key drill. It isolates the initiation phase so you can practise it without the rest of the rep.

  1. Hang from the bar with an overhand grip, arms straight, body still. Let the shoulders rise toward your ears. This is the passive hang.
  2. Without bending your elbows, pull your shoulder blades down and slightly together. Your body rises a few centimeters. Your neck gets longer.
  3. Hold the top for one to two seconds. Feel the muscles under your armpits and in the lower middle back working, not your arms.
  4. Lower with control until the shoulders are back up by your ears. That is one rep.

Start with 3 sets of 5–8 reps, two or three times a week, at the start of your pulling session. Progress by adding a longer hold at the top, then more reps, up to 3 sets of 12. Common mistakes: bending the elbows, swinging, holding your breath, and shrugging at the top instead of staying long in the neck. If a full hang is too much for your grip yet, do the same movement with your feet on a box or on a low bar, as in band-assisted pull-ups.

Other drills that cover the rest of the scapular muscles:

  • Prone Y and T raises. Lie face down. Lift the arms in a Y (thumbs up) for the lower trapezius, then in a T for the middle trapezius and rhomboids. Light or no weight, 2–3 sets of 10–12, slow.
  • Face pulls. Band or cable at face height. Pull toward your forehead, elbows high, shoulder blades down and back. 2–3 sets of 12–15.
  • Band pull-aparts. Arms straight in front, pull the band apart to your chest. Keep the shoulders down. 2–3 sets of 15–20.
  • Serratus wall slides and push-up plus. For the serratus anterior. Slide your forearms up a wall while pushing the blades forward around the rib cage, or finish a push-up by pressing the floor away until the upper back rounds slightly. 2 sets of 8–12.
  • Australian rows (inverted rows). Horizontal pulling trains retraction under load. Finish each rep with the shoulder blades together and the shoulders away from the ears. They also sit in the first pull-up guide as a core progression.

Problems and injury notes: winging, tight neck, impingement

Winging scapula. If the inner edge of a shoulder blade lifts away from the ribs when you push against a wall, that is called winging. The classic cause is weakness or injury of the serratus anterior, often through the long thoracic nerve, which runs along the side of the chest and can be irritated by trauma, heavy straps or repetitive overhead work[3]. Mild winging from an under-trained serratus often improves with the drills above. Winging that appeared suddenly, after an injury, or with numbness or clear weakness needs a clinician.

Upper-trap dominance and a tight neck. If your neck and upper shoulders ache after pull-ups, the most common story is that the upper trapezius and levator scapulae did the lower trapezius's job. Both attach to the neck, so they pull on it every rep. Reduce volume, go back to scapular pull-ups and active hangs, and keep the shoulders down in every set. Persistent neck pain, headaches or pain that spreads down the arm should be checked.

Impingement risk. When the scapula does not rotate upward enough or tips forward, the space under the roof of the shoulder gets smaller and tendons can get pinched, especially at the bottom and top of the rep. The consensus literature links scapular dyskinesis with impingement symptoms without claiming it is the single cause[5]. Pain at the front or side of the shoulder that gets worse with reps, or that keeps you awake, is a reason to stop and get assessed rather than push through.

When to see a clinician. Sudden winging, pain with numbness or tingling, a shoulder that feels unstable or clicks painfully, or symptoms that have not improved after two to three weeks of reduced load. This article is for training, not for diagnosis.

How do you program scapular work?

Keep it simple. Put scapular pull-ups and one row or face-pull drill at the start of each pulling session as a warm-up, two or three times a week, and add a serratus drill on one of those days. Count them as skill work: short sets, no grinding, stop when the shoulders start to creep up. From there the main plan takes over; see the pull-up workout plan for a weekly structure and the muscles worked overview for how the scapular muscles fit with the lats and arms. Frequently asked questions

What are scapular pull-ups?

Straight-arm pulls from the bar where you only move the shoulder blades down and together, lifting the body a few centimeters. They train the first phase of a pull-up, mainly the lower trapezius.

Why does my neck hurt after pull-ups?

Usually because the upper trapezius and levator scapulae are lifting the shoulder blades toward the ears instead of the lower trapezius pulling them down. Reduce volume, practise scapular pull-ups and keep the shoulders down. If the pain persists or spreads into the arm, see a clinician.

Should I pinch my shoulder blades together at the top of a pull-up?

Aim for a firm, controlled retraction with the shoulders away from the ears, not a maximal pinch. Over-squeezing often turns into a shrug.

What is winging scapula?

The inner edge of the shoulder blade lifting off the rib cage, typically because the serratus anterior is weak or poorly innervated (long thoracic nerve). Mild cases often respond to serratus training; sudden or painful winging needs a clinician.

How many scapular pull-ups should I do per day?

Two or three times a week is enough: 3 sets of 5–12 at the start of a pulling session. Daily is fine if the sets stay easy and the shoulders stay down.
