Should you train pull-ups to failure?

What the meta-analyses show and how many reps to leave in reserve·7 min read

Usually not. Studies that compare sets taken to failure with sets stopped a rep or two short find similar gains in strength and muscle, so failure is not required. For most pull-up sets, aim to finish with one to three clean reps still in the tank, and keep true failure for the occasional last set or a test.

Should you train pull-ups to failure?

Usually not. Failure here means momentary muscular failure: the point where you try your hardest and cannot finish the next rep. Stopping a rep or two before that point gives strength and muscle gains that look about the same in the studies we found, and it is easier to repeat week after week.[1][2]

The question is sharper for pull-ups than for most lifts. You cannot take two kilos off your body halfway through a set, so the only way to control effort is to choose when you stop, or to change the variation with a band, a slower tempo or extra weight. Coaches describe that choice in reps in reserve (RIR): 0 RIR means you went to failure, 1 RIR means you could have done one more clean rep, 2 RIR means two more.

What does the research say about failure versus stopping short?

Two systematic reviews with meta-analysis are the best evidence we found. Each pooled 15 trials of resistance training in healthy adults. They cover resistance training in general, and we found no trial that compared pull-ups taken to failure with pull-ups stopped short.

The first, by Grgic and colleagues, included only young adults. Across its trials there was no significant difference between failure and non-failure training for strength (effect size -0.09, 95% confidence interval -0.22 to 0.05) or for hypertrophy, meaning muscle size (0.22, interval -0.11 to 0.55). The authors concluded that training to failure does not seem to be required for gains in strength and muscle size, and that it does not seem to harm them either.[1]

Two details in the same paper matter. When the groups did not do equal training volume, non-failure training was favoured for strength (effect size -0.32, interval -0.57 to -0.07). In the subgroup of resistance-trained people, failure training had a small edge for hypertrophy (0.15, interval 0.03 to 0.26).[1]

The second review, by Refalo and colleagues, looked only at hypertrophy. Using any definition of set failure it found a trivial advantage for failure (0.19, interval 0.00 to 0.37). When it kept only studies of true momentary failure, there was no advantage either way (0.12, interval -0.13 to 0.37). Stopping a set at a higher or a moderate velocity loss, which is how much the bar slows during the set, made no difference either. The authors point to a possible non-linear relationship: getting closer to failure does not always bring more growth.[2]

Read these results with their limits in mind. The Grgic review included only young adults, and the effects are small. Nothing here tells you what happens over years of pull-up training.

What are reps in reserve, and can you judge them?

The RIR-based effort scale ties a number to the count of reps you have left: RPE 10 is 0 RIR, RPE 9 is 1 RIR, and so on. In a study of 29 squatters, novice and experienced, the rating tracked bar speed closely, with slower reps rated harder.[3]

The catch is that people misjudge how much is left. In 48 adults with recreational training experience, the error in their estimate of reps to failure on the first set was 2.0 reps on the chest press and 3.1 on the leg press. By the third set the error was 0.6 and 1.6, and one session of practice barely improved it.[4] In people with at least a year of training, a separate study found they typically underestimated how many reps they could do by about 2 (interval 0 to 4).[5]

Both studies used machines, not pull-ups, so treat the size of the error as a rough guide. The direction is useful, though: when you feel you have one rep left, you often have more. That is a reason to stop a set based on form and speed rather than on a gut count alone. Signs that a pull-up set is nearly done:

  • The pull slows clearly and the last inch takes a grind.
  • Your chin only just clears the bar, or you stop short of a full hang at the bottom.
  • You start to swing, kick or shrug your shoulders toward your ears.

These cues come from coaching practice, not from a pull-up trial. Our pull-up mistakes page shows what clean form looks like.

What does training to failure cost you?

The main measured cost is recovery. In ten resistance-trained men doing bench press and squat, sets taken to failure slowed the recovery of jump height and bar speed for up to 24 to 48 hours, compared with the same total volume stopped short of failure.[6] Pull-ups were not tested.

That matters because most people want to train pull-ups several times a week. If one session leaves you slower the next day, your following sets are weaker, and total weekly reps can drop. Our page on how often to do pull-ups covers how to space sessions. We found no study that measured elbow or shoulder problems in failure versus non-failure pull-up training, so we cannot say failure raises that risk. It is a reasonable caution, not a finding.

How close to failure should you stop on pull-ups?

No pull-up trial sets a number, so the table below is a practical suggestion built on the evidence above, not a result.

Your levelWhere to stop most setsWhy
No pull-up yet (hangs, negatives, band)When form changes or the lowering speeds upYour RIR guess is least reliable here; use form as the stop sign
1 to 5 strict repsAbout 1 rep short, with extra sets for volumeWith few reps each rep is a large share of your max
6 or more strict reps1 to 3 reps in reserve; last set 0 to 1Gains are similar to failure, recovery is better

If your max is only one or two reps, a set to failure is one or two reps anyway. Build volume with more sets, band-assisted reps or negative pull-ups instead of grinding single reps.

When does going to failure make sense?

  • The last set of a session. You pay the recovery cost only once, and there is nothing after it to ruin.
  • Test days. A max-rep set tells you where you stand and helps you set the next block. Do it fresh, with clean form.
  • Learning your own limit. One bodyweight set to failure, now and then, recalibrates your RIR guess, given the errors above.
  • If you are well trained and chasing size. The resistance-trained subgroup showed a small edge for failure.[1]

Avoid failure on your first sets, on consecutive days, and on weighted pull-ups, where a missed rep with a load is harder to bail out of. That last point is practical caution, not a studied result.

How do you apply this in your next session?

  1. Test your strict max once, fresh, with good form.
  2. Work most sets at 1 to 3 reps under that max, and use the speed and form cues above to stop.
  3. Log reps and how the set felt. If reps climb without more effort, you are progressing.
  4. Add reps before you add sets, and sets before you add days. See the pull-up plateau page if progress stalls.
  5. Keep one set per session, at most, for failure.

Frequently asked questions

Do you need to train pull-ups to failure to build muscle?

No. A meta-analysis of 15 trials found no significant difference in strength or muscle size between failure and non-failure training, and a second found no hypertrophy advantage for true momentary failure. Neither tested pull-ups specifically.

How many reps should you leave in reserve on pull-ups?

One to three on most sets is a sensible range, with the last set at zero to one. No pull-up study sets this number, so treat it as a practical suggestion based on the general research.

Is training to failure bad for you?

The reviews found it does not harm strength or size gains, but failure sets slowed recovery of jump height and bar speed for 24 to 48 hours in one study. We found no study of injury rates in failure pull-up training.

How can you tell you are close to failure with only a few reps?

Watch speed and form: a clearly slower pull, a chin that barely clears the bar, swinging or shrugging. People tend to underestimate their reps left by about 2, so one occasional test set helps calibrate.

Should beginners train pull-ups to failure?

Not as a rule. With a max of one or two reps, build volume through assisted reps, negatives and more sets, and stop when form changes. Failure matters less than steady, repeatable practice.
