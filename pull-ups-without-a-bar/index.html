Pull-ups without a bar: what you can train

Build pulling strength at home, and know when a real bar is worth getting·8 min read

You cannot do a true pull-up without something to hang from, but you can build most of the pulling strength it needs with a table, a towel, a door or a backpack. Rows have the closest evidence; the other options are reasonable but unproven for a first pull-up. The plan below uses what you have and says honestly where carryover is a guess.

Can you train for pull-ups without a bar?

Partly. A pull-up needs a fixed point above your head that holds your body weight, so the exact movement is not possible without one. What you can train is the pulling muscles and the grip: the lats, the upper back, the elbow flexors and the forearms. Those are the muscles that do the work in a pull-up[2].

We found no trial that tested bar-free training for a first pull-up, so treat everything here as a sensible bridge, not a proven replacement. If you have a safe place to hang, even once or twice a week, the hang itself is the most specific practice you can add.

What can you hang from safely?

Before any of the exercises, check what you could hang from. A bar-free week is often a bar-found week.

  • Playground or park bars. Fixed, usually well built and free. Check for rust, wobble and slippery metal before you trust your weight to it.
  • A sturdy tree branch. Thick, living wood, close to the trunk. Dead or thin branches break without warning.
  • A gym. A single visit a week to hang and do negatives can carry a lot of the plan.

Whatever you choose, run the same safety check: hang with a little weight first (feet on the floor), bounce gently, then lift your feet only if nothing moves or creaks. Never hang from a shelf, a pipe, a banister or a door frame that is not rated for it. Our home pull-up bar guide covers the bars that are built for this.

Which exercises work without a bar?

Under-table rows

Lie under a heavy, stable table, grip the edge with an overhand grip and pull your chest toward it, body straight. Make sure the table cannot tip or slide and that the edge will not bite into your hands. This is the same pattern as a bodyweight row. Studies of inverted rows show activity in the lats, rhomboids and trapezius along with the spinal stabilisers[3][4], which are the same group a pull-up asks for.

Towel door rows

Loop a thick towel around both sides of a door handle, close a sturdy door that opens away from you, lean back with straight arms and row yourself toward the door. Check the door is latched and the towel will not slip. Make the exercise harder by stepping your feet closer to the door. Stop if you feel the door give.

Backpack rows

Fill a backpack with books, hinge forward and row it to your lower ribs, one arm at a time or both. It trains the same muscles with a different angle, and you can add load gradually. Keep your back flat and the weight light enough for clean reps.

Towel isometric pulls

Hold a towel with both hands at chest height and pull the ends apart or pull against a fixed loop for 10 seconds. It trains tension, not range of motion. Isometric work builds strength mostly near the joint angle you train[5], so treat it as a supplement.

Grip work

Towel wring-outs, carrying a heavy bag and holding the heavy backpack by the straps build grip and forearms. Grip is a common limiter on early dead hangs.

Which options carry over best?

The honest answer is that nobody has tested these against each other for a first pull-up. Here is our reading of the evidence we found, not a ranking from a study.

Rows come out on top because they are heavy enough to be a real stimulus and they work the same muscles. Still, a row is a horizontal pull and a pull-up is a vertical one, so expect the last stretch to need real practice on a bar.

What mistakes should you avoid?

  • Sagging hips on rows. Keep your body in one line from head to heels, otherwise the row turns into an easy hip-assisted pull.
  • Shrugging. Pull your shoulder blades back and down before your elbows bend, not up toward your ears.
  • Rushing the reps. Take a second to reach the end of the pull and lower slowly. Slow lowering is also practice for negatives later.
  • Only doing the easy angle. When a row feels easy, move your feet closer to the anchor instead of adding endless reps.
  • Ignoring pain. Sharp pain in an elbow or shoulder is a reason to stop, not to push through.

A 3-day no-bar plan

Do this on non-consecutive days and keep a rep or two in reserve on every set[1]. Warm up your shoulders and wrists for a few minutes first.

  1. Day 1, rows. Under-table or towel door rows, 3 sets of 6 to 10 reps. Rest two minutes between sets.
  2. Day 2, backpack and grip. Backpack rows, 3 sets of 8 each side, then a 30-second towel hold or bag carry, twice.
  3. Day 3, hang day. If you can reach a real bar this week, do 4 short dead hangs (see dead hang benefits) and 2 sets of towel isometric pulls. If not, repeat day 1 with a harder angle.

Add one rep a week to the rows. When 3 sets of 10 feel easy, shift your feet closer to make the row harder, or add weight to the backpack.

When should you buy a bar?

Buy one when you have trained rows consistently for three or four weeks, you want to practise hangs and negatives, and you have a safe place to mount it. A doorway bar, a wall mount and a free-standing frame suit different homes; the guide linked above compares them and what to check before you install one.

If a bar is not an option, a visit to a park or gym once a week is a good compromise. After that, the first pull-up guide and band-assisted pull-ups take you from hanging to a first rep.

Frequently asked questions

Can you build a pull-up without a bar?

You can build much of the pulling strength with rows, backpack work and grip training, but the final skill needs a bar to hang from. We found no trial that tested bar-free training for a first pull-up.

What can I use instead of a pull-up bar?

Under-table rows, towel door rows, backpack rows and towel isometric pulls train the same muscles. A playground bar, a sturdy tree branch or a gym gives you something real to hang from.

Are inverted rows as good as pull-ups?

Not the same movement. Inverted rows train the lats and upper back with a horizontal pull and show similar muscle activity to pull-up variations, but they do not replace hanging and pulling vertically.

Is it safe to hang from a door frame or tree?

Only if it is built for your weight. Test with your feet on the floor first, avoid thin or dead branches, and never hang from a shelf, a pipe or a door frame that is not rated for it.

How often should I train without a bar?

Two to three sessions a week on non-consecutive days suit most beginners. Keep a rep or two in reserve and add a rep each week.
