Kipping vs strict pull-ups: what changes, what the research says and which to learn first

A comparison for people building toward their first pull-up·8 min read

A strict pull-up uses only your pulling muscles, while a kipping pull-up adds a hip swing that moves part of the work to your trunk and legs. Studies show the kip lowers lat and biceps activity, and CrossFit injury surveys list the shoulder as the most injured area, although no study proves the kip itself is the cause. If you are chasing a first pull-up, train strict and add a kip later only if your sport asks for it.

What separates a strict, kipping and butterfly pull-up?

A strict pull-up starts from a hang with straight arms and ends with your chin over the bar. Only the pulling muscles do the work and the body stays almost still. A kipping pull-up adds a swing: you move from a hollow body position to an arched one and use that momentum to help your chin rise. The butterfly pull-up is a faster, continuous version in which the hips and legs travel in a circle, so one rep flows into the next with no pause at the bottom.[3]

Which muscles and joints work harder in each style?

In a strict rep the latissimus dorsi, a broad flat muscle over the lower back, pulls the upper arm down toward your trunk.[1] The biceps brachii and brachialis bend the elbow, the lower trapezius and serratus anterior steer the shoulder blade, and the rotator cuff keeps the ball of the upper arm centred in its socket. Your abdominals and hips mostly hold the body still.

A kip hands part of that work to the trunk and hips. In an EMG and motion-capture study of eleven athletes, adding a kip raised the activity of the rectus abdominis, external oblique, iliopsoas and tensor fasciae latae, and lowered the activity of the biceps brachii. Peak hip angle was about 49 degrees larger in the kipping rep.[2] A second study of eleven participants compared strict, kipping and butterfly reps. Lat activity was lower in the pulling phase of the kip and the lowering phase of the butterfly, biceps activity was lower in the butterfly, and the rectus femoris, gluteus maximus and rectus abdominis worked harder in both kipping styles.[3]

So a kip trains a coordinated whole-body swing and lets you string reps together with less upper-body fatigue. It also gives your lats and biceps less of the job. The authors of the first study put it this way: pick a strict rep for higher upper-body activation, or a kipping rep for full-body activation and potentially more reps.[2]

At the shoulder the difference is timing. A strict rep is slow, so the cuff and lats control every degree. In a kip the body swings down and forward and the muscles around the shoulder have to brake that momentum at the bottom, with the arm overhead. That is a reasoned description of the mechanics, not a measured result from these studies. Our rotator cuff guide covers the joint anatomy in detail.

What does the research say about kipping and shoulder injuries?

The short answer is that we could not find a study comparing shoulder injury rates between people who do strict pull-ups and people who kip at the same volume. What exists is CrossFit injury surveillance, and most of it relies on surveys with self-reported injuries.

A systematic review of 25 studies and 12,079 CrossFit participants found a mean injury prevalence of 35.3 percent and an incidence between 0.2 and 18.9 per 1000 training hours, similar to weightlifting and powerlifting. The shoulder was the most injured area (26 percent), ahead of the spine (24 percent) and the knee (18 percent). The reviewers said study quality was too limited for solid conclusions about risk factors.[6]

A survey of 187 CrossFit athletes, a 19 percent response rate, found that 44 had a shoulder injury in the previous six months. The rate was 1.94 per 1000 training hours, which the authors judged comparable to other recreational exercise. Kipping pull-ups were named as the main cause in 5 of the reported injuries and butterfly pull-ups in 2, behind overhead presses and snatches. The authors flagged sampling bias and self-reporting as limits.[4]

A review of upper-limb injuries in CrossFit describes the kipping pull-up as loading the shoulder in a high degree of abduction and external rotation, and it advises beginners, intermediate athletes and anyone with a previous injury to be careful with kipping pull-ups. The same review admits there is no granular data on the mechanism behind each exercise's injuries.[5]

Strict pull-ups are not exempt. A motion-capture study of eleven people found that high arm elevation narrows the space under the acromion, and that wide-grip and reverse-grip pull-ups showed patterns linked with higher impingement risk. It did not test kipping.[7] Taken together, the kip is a plausible shoulder stressor and a common injury context, but the evidence cannot show that the kip itself is the cause. Treat it as a skill that needs a strength base, not as something proven dangerous or proven safe.

Who benefits from which style?

  • Beginners and anyone chasing a first pull-up: strict. It trains the lats and biceps directly, and that is the strength you are missing.[2]
  • Strength and muscle goals: strict, because upper-body activation was higher than in the kip.[2][3]
  • CrossFit-style athletes and classes that program kipping reps: a kip, once a strict base is in place, because it can allow more reps through lower upper-body fatigue.[2]
  • Current or past shoulder trouble: strict, slow and pain-free, with advice from a clinician. A previous injury is one reason reviewers give for extra care.[5]
  • Anyone who wants general strength for climbing, sport or health: strict covers it. The kip is a specialised skill.

What should you be able to do before you learn a kip?

No study sets a threshold, so this checklist is a coaching convention and not a tested rule.

  • Several clean strict pull-ups with a controlled lowering. A handful, around five, is a common coaching benchmark.
  • An active hang where you can set the shoulder blades down and hold without pain.
  • A pain-free overhead reach, with no pinch at the front or side of the shoulder.
  • A hollow body hold and an arch hold on the floor, so you can make both shapes on purpose.
  • No unresolved shoulder pain. If it hurts, see a clinician before you add speed.

Then learn the kip as its own skill. Practise the swing with straight arms first, add the pull later and end the set before form breaks down.

How do you build strict strength first?

Progressive overload is the rule. The ACSM position stand recommends 2 to 3 training days a week for novices and load increases of 2 to 10 percent once you can do one or two reps over your target.[8] For pull-ups that becomes a ladder like this.

  1. Active hangs and scapular pulls. Learn to set the shoulder blades before the elbows bend.
  2. Band-assisted pull-ups. Use thinner bands over the weeks. See the band-assisted guide.
  3. Slow lowerings from the top. Take three to five seconds on the way down.
  4. Rows. Inverted or dumbbell rows add pulling volume while your arms stay in front of you.
  5. Short strict sets. Do sets of one to three clean reps with full rests and add a rep when they feel smooth.

Which style should you choose for a first pull-up?

Choose strict. A first pull-up is a test of pulling strength, and the kip gives the lats and biceps less of the work.[2][3] A swing can also lift your chin over the bar before the strength is there, which hides what still needs training. It adds speed to a shoulder that may not yet have the strength to brake it, which is reasoning and not a measured result. Once you can do a few strict reps, you can decide whether a kip fits your goals. If you never train in a format that asks for one, you lose nothing by skipping it.

Which mistakes show up most?

  • Kipping to reach a first rep. You get a rep without the strength that makes later reps possible.
  • Treating the kip as a bigger pull-up. It is a different skill in which hips, trunk and arms have to time together.
  • Swinging with bent arms. Keep the arms straight and active during the swing, then add the pull.
  • Dropping into the bottom. Control the descent so muscles catch you and not the joint capsule.
  • Chasing reps while tired. Improper form was the most commonly named cause of shoulder injuries in the CrossFit survey, tied with a flare-up of an older injury.[4] Stop a rep early.
  • Ignoring pain. A pinch in the shoulder is a reason to stop and get it checked. See our guide to pull-up mistakes for form errors.

Frequently asked questions

Are kipping pull-ups bad for your shoulders?

We found no study showing that kipping itself causes shoulder injury, and CrossFit shoulder injury rates are reported as comparable to other recreational exercise. Kipping pull-ups do appear among reported causes and reviews call them high-risk, so build a strict base first and stop if something pinches.

Do kipping pull-ups count as real pull-ups?

They follow a different standard. A kip uses hip and trunk momentum and less lat and biceps activity, so it is a legitimate skill in CrossFit-style workouts but does not test pulling strength the way a strict rep does.

Can I learn kipping pull-ups without a strict pull-up?

It is not recommended. No study sets a threshold, but coaches commonly ask for a handful of strict reps first so the swing adds to your strength instead of replacing it.

What is a butterfly pull-up?

A continuous, circular version of the kip in which the hips and legs cycle so reps flow without a pause. Evidence is limited to a small EMG study, and it is an advanced skill that is not meant for beginners.

Will kipping help me get my first pull-up?

Not as a strength builder. Studies show lower lat and biceps activity in the kip, so strict progressions such as bands, slow lowerings and rows train the strength you need.
