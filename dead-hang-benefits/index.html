Dead hang benefits: what it does, how long to hang and how to progress

Active vs passive hangs, timing and progression·6 min read

A dead hang means hanging from a bar with straight arms and no movement. It builds grip strength and shoulder familiarity under load, which are the foundations of a pull-up, and it is simple enough to do almost anywhere you have a bar.

What is a dead hang?

A dead hang is exactly what it sounds like: you hold the bar, let your body hang with arms straight, and stay there for time. There are two main versions. In a passive hang you relax into the shoulders and let them rise toward your ears. In an active hang you gently pull your shoulder blades down and back, so your shoulders stay engaged while your arms stay straight.

The active hang is the position a pull-up starts from, and it's the first of the five moves in our first pull-up guide.

What are the benefits of dead hangs?

Dead hangs are simple, but they train several things that matter for pulling:

  • Grip endurance. If your hands let go before your back gets tired, grip is the limiter. Hanging builds it directly.
  • Comfort under load. Hanging teaches your shoulders and elbows to tolerate your body weight in the stretched position.
  • Shoulder awareness. Practising an active hang helps you feel the scapular depression that starts a clean rep.
  • Time under tension on your forearms. The muscles of your hands and forearms work for the whole set.
  • A low-skill, low-fatigue habit. Because it involves no pulling, you can add hangs to most sessions without wrecking your recovery.

These are practical training effects. Claims about hanging fixing posture or curing pain are common online, but they aren't something we promise. Treat a dead hang as strength practice, not as treatment.

How long should you dead hang?

There is no magic number. A useful way to think about it is by level:

Aiming for about 30 seconds in an active hang is a good first goal before you start chasing reps. Longer than that has diminishing returns for pull-ups; once your grip is no longer the limit, shift your effort to pulling movements.

Active hang vs passive hang: which should you do?

Do both, but know why. The active hang is the most specific to pull-ups because it teaches the shoulder position you need at the bottom of a rep. The passive hang lets you relax into a stretch and is easier on your grip and muscles, so it is a good way to finish a session. If you're short on time, favour active hangs.

How do I do a dead hang properly?

  1. Grip the bar firmly. Use a full grip, thumbs around the bar, hands about shoulder-width apart.
  2. Step off or lift your feet. Take your weight smoothly rather than dropping into it.
  3. Set your shoulders. For an active hang, gently draw your shoulder blades down and back. Keep your arms straight.
  4. Keep your body quiet. Ribs down, legs together, no swinging.
  5. Breathe. Don't hold your breath; steady breathing helps you last longer.
  6. Step down before form breaks. End the set when your shoulders shrug up or your grip starts to slip.

How do I progress dead hangs?

  • Add time. Add 5 seconds when you hit your target in two sessions in a row.
  • Add sets before time. Three clean 20-second hangs usually beat one shaky 40.
  • Move into scapular pulls. From the hang, pull your shoulder blades down without bending your arms. This is the first movement of a rep.
  • Try one-arm assisted holds later. Only once the basics are solid.

When you can hang comfortably, move on to negatives and band-assisted pull-ups. Your weekly plan can keep a short hang at the start of every session as a warm-up.

When should you stop or skip a dead hang?

Hanging should feel like effort in your hands and forearms, not pinching in the shoulders. Stop if you feel sharp pain, pinching, numbness or tingling in the shoulder, elbow or hands, and don't push through it. Previous or current shoulder issues are a reason to talk to a physiotherapist before loading a hang. PullUp Master is a training tool, not medical advice.

Dead hangs and your first pull-up

Dead hangs rarely win a pull-up alone, but they remove one of the commonest roadblocks: grip that gives up first. Pair them with the movement patterns in the guide, and check your form against the common pull-up mistakes as you progress. Frequently asked questions

How long should a beginner dead hang?

Start with three sets of 10 to 20 seconds and build toward about 30 seconds in an active hang. Add a few seconds once you hit your target in two sessions.

Is a dead hang good for shoulders?

It can help your shoulders get used to supporting your body weight, but it is not a treatment. If you feel sharp pain, pinching or numbness, stop and see a physiotherapist.

What is the difference between an active and a passive hang?

In an active hang you pull your shoulder blades gently down and back while keeping your arms straight. In a passive hang you let your shoulders relax up toward your ears.

How often can I do dead hangs?

Because hangs involve no pulling, most people can do them in most sessions. Keep them short and clean, and cut back if your hands, elbows or shoulders feel irritated.
