Lat pulldown vs pull-up: what the research shows and how to move from one to the other

An evidence check for people training toward pull-ups·8 min read

A lat pulldown and a pull-up train the same main muscles: the lats, the biceps and the muscles around the shoulder blade. EMG studies show similar lat activity in both, but the pull-up adds trunk work and asks you to move your whole body. The pulldown is a sensible stepping stone while you cannot pull yourself up, and the evidence on how well it carries over is small.

What is the difference between a lat pulldown and a pull-up?

In both exercises the lats pull the upper arm down and back toward the trunk. The muscle starts on the lower thoracic spine, the thoracolumbar fascia, the back of the pelvis and the lower ribs, and it ends on the front of the upper arm bone.[1] The difference is which end of the chain moves.

FeatureLat pulldownPull-up
Chain typeOpen: the hands move, the body is held in place by seat and thigh padClosed: the hands are fixed, the body moves toward them
LoadWeight stack, chosen in small stepsYour body weight, changed only by assistance or added weight
Trunk and legsSupported by the seat and padHanging free, so you control them yourself

Physiotherapists call the pulldown an open-chain exercise and the pull-up a closed-chain exercise. Pozzi and colleagues compared six matched pairs of closed-chain and open-chain upper-limb exercises in 22 healthy young adults. The closed-chain versions produced more activity in the infraspinatus, lower trapezius, erector spinae and external oblique.[2] The abstract does not list a pull-up versus pulldown pair, so read it as a general principle about fixed versus free hands, not as a measurement of these two lifts.

What do EMG studies show about the two exercises?

The most direct comparison is by Doma and colleagues, who compared chin-ups (palms toward you) with the lat pulldown.[3] The lats were among the most active muscles in both exercises. During the pulling phase, the biceps and the erector spinae were more active in the chin-up, and shoulder and neck-base movement was larger. During the lowering phase, the rectus abdominis was more active in the pulldown. The authors concluded that the chin-up looks like the more functional exercise.

Keep three limits in mind.

  • That study tested chin-ups, not the palms-away pull-up.
  • Surface EMG tells you how active a muscle was in a given rep. It does not tell you how much strength or size you will gain.
  • These studies use small groups of healthy volunteers, mostly men, at loads the researchers chose.

Andersen and colleagues had 15 men do six-repetition-maximum sets with grips of 1, 1.5 and 2 times their shoulder-point distance. Activation of the lats, trapezius and infraspinatus was similar across widths over the whole rep. The widest grip allowed about 3 kg less load, and it gave more lat activity than the narrow grip in the lowering phase only.[4] Lusk and colleagues tested 12 men and found that a palms-away grip activated the lats more than palms-toward at both widths.[5] Sperandei and colleagues compared three pulldown styles in 24 trained men. The lats did not differ between styles, but the authors recommended the bar in front of the neck and advised avoiding the behind-the-neck version.[6]

Why is the pulldown easier to scale than the pull-up?

On the machine you choose the weight in small steps, stop the set when form slips and add load or reps without changing the movement. A pull-up fixes the load at your body weight. You can reduce it with a band or add to it with a belt, but the steps are coarse.

The load you use matters for planning. Sanchez-Moreno and colleagues tested 25 trained men. Maximum pull-up repetitions correlated with pulldown repetitions at a load equal to body mass (r = 0.62). They did not correlate with the pulldown one-repetition maximum (r = 0.09) or with repetitions at 80% of it (r = -0.15). Heavier and leaner bodies also did fewer pull-ups.[7] A reasonable reading is that repetitions with a load near your body weight say more about pull-up ability than a heavy single does. The study was correlational, small and limited to trained athletes, so it does not prove that raising one number raises the other.

Does pulldown training carry over to pull-ups?

Some, with weak evidence. Li and colleagues randomised 34 recreationally active men to eight weeks of lat pulldown training on a stable or an unstable set-up. Pull-up endurance rose in both groups, and the unstable group gained more.[8] The study had no group that skipped training, so it cannot separate the pulldown effect from practice on the test itself. It also says nothing about people who cannot yet do one pull-up.

Among the studies checked for this page, none compared pulldowns with negatives or assisted pull-ups for getting a first rep. The closed-chain principle and the chin-up EMG both point toward practising the real movement as soon as you can. Treat the pulldown as a supplement that builds pulling volume and not as a replacement.

When is the pulldown a useful stepping stone, and when should you switch?

The pulldown earns its place in these cases:

  • You cannot yet hang and pull your chin to the bar, even with a band.
  • You want extra pulling volume after pull-up sets, or a load you can adjust in small steps.

Switch the main focus to pull-up variations when pulldowns at a stack weight near your body weight feel solid for sets of about eight to ten. That threshold is a coaching rule of thumb, not a study result. Stack weight is also only a marker, because the thigh pad holds you down and the bar path differs from a pull-up. Keep the pulldown afterwards as an accessory exercise.

How do you do a lat pulldown properly?

  1. Lock in. Set the thigh pad snug so your legs hold you down. Choose a weight you can move without leaning back to cheat.
  2. Grip. Hold the bar palms away, thumbs wrapped, hands between shoulder width and about twice that. Across 1 to 2 times shoulder-point distance, lat activation looked similar in the study above, so pick the width where your elbows travel straight down.[4]
  3. Torso angle. Sit tall with the chest up and ribs down. Lean back only slightly and keep that angle through the whole rep.
  4. Start the pull. From fully extended arms, pull the shoulder blades down first, then drive the elbows down and back until the bar reaches your upper chest.
  5. Bar in front. Pull to the front of the neck and chest, never behind the head.[6]
  6. Return slowly. Let the bar rise over two to three seconds until your arms are straight again. Your shoulders can lift a little at the top. That is the full range.
  7. No swinging. Hips stay down and the bar does not bounce. If you need momentum, lower the weight.

Which mistakes make the pulldown less useful?

  • Too much weight. You lean back into a row and the movement stops resembling a pull-up.
  • Short range. Cutting the top of the rep leaves the lats unstretched, so you train the middle of the movement only.
  • Pulling with the hands. Think about driving the elbows toward your hips instead of curling the bar down.
  • Behind the neck. The EMG study found it a poor choice for the lats.[6]
  • Chasing the stack. A heavier pulldown max did not track pull-up reps in the study above, so judge progress by controlled reps near body weight.[7]

Frequently asked questions

Is the lat pulldown as good as the pull-up for the lats?

EMG shows the lats are highly active in both, so for the lats alone they look similar. The pull-up adds trunk work and asks you to control your whole body, and the chin-up study found higher biceps and erector spinae activity than the pulldown. Evidence on long-term strength or size differences is thin.

Can lat pulldowns alone get me to my first pull-up?

They may help, because pulldown training raised pull-up endurance in recreationally active men in one trial. No study checked for this page tested pulldowns alone for a first pull-up, so add negatives or assisted pull-ups as soon as you can.

How heavy should I pulldown before I try pull-ups?

A common coaching target is clean sets of eight to ten with a stack weight near your body weight. That figure is a rule of thumb. The correlation study found reps at body-mass load tracked pull-ups better than a heavy one-rep maximum.

Should I use a wide or narrow grip on the pulldown?

Pick the width where your elbows move straight down and the shoulders feel comfortable. In a study of 15 men, grips from 1 to 2 times shoulder-point distance gave similar lat activation. A palms-away grip activated the lats more than palms-toward in another study.

Is the behind-the-neck pulldown a good idea?

The EMG study that compared styles recommended the bar in front and advised avoiding behind the neck, because it was a poor choice for the lats. Nothing in the evidence here gives a reason to prefer it, so pull to the upper chest.
