How long should you rest between pull-up sets?

What the rest-interval research shows and how to use it·8 min read

Rest about 2 to 3 minutes between hard, low-rep pull-up sets, and about 90 seconds to 2 minutes for moderate-rep sets aimed at muscle. Reviews of resistance training find that rests beyond 2 minutes help trained people maximise strength, while muscle growth looks similar once you rest more than about 90 seconds. We found no trial that tested rest times on pull-ups specifically, so treat these ranges as a well-supported starting point, not a rule.

How long should you rest between pull-up sets?

Use this table as a starting point. The ranges come from reviews and position stands on resistance training in general. They were not tested on pull-ups.

Our practical reading: 2 minutes is a sensible default for most pull-up work, and up to 3 minutes is reasonable for your hardest, lowest-rep sets. That is a suggestion built on the ranges above, not a tested pull-up protocol.

What does the research say about rest and strength?

In an 8-week trial, 21 resistance-trained men trained the whole body three times a week with 3 sets of 8 to 12 repetition maximum. One group rested 1 minute between sets and the other 3 minutes. The 3-minute group gained more maximal strength in the squat and bench press, and more thickness in the front of the thigh. The difference in the triceps was a trend (p = 0.06). Gains in upper-body muscle endurance were similar.[1]

A systematic review of 23 studies with 491 participants looked at strength gains across rest lengths. Robust gains were possible even with rests under 60 seconds. Rests longer than 2 minutes seemed necessary to maximise strength in trained people, while 60 to 120 seconds looked sufficient for untrained people.[2]

The ACSM position stand points the same way for heavy work: for experienced lifters training with 1 to 6 repetition maximum loads, it recommends 3 to 5 minutes between sets.[6] A review of the factors that set rest length adds that, for loads below 90 percent of one-repetition maximum, 3 to 5 minutes lets you keep training intensity from set to set.[7]

What does the research say about rest and muscle growth?

The old idea was that muscle growth needs short rests. A 2014 review found no long-term study in which shorter rests produced more growth than longer rests, and one study found the opposite. It concluded that the literature does not support the claim that hypertrophy needs shorter rests than strength training, or that fixed rest times beat resting by feel.[4]

A 2017 systematic review of six studies called the findings equivocal. Both short and long rests can work, but the better-measured studies in trained people hinted at an advantage for long rests, and the authors asked for more research.[3]

The most recent analysis pooled 19 measurements from 9 studies. Effects overlapped heavily across rest lengths, with large differences between studies. The authors saw a small benefit for resting longer than 60 seconds, possibly because it protects the volume you can lift, and no appreciable difference beyond about 90 seconds. For the arm, the estimate leaned slightly toward longer rests but the uncertainty range included zero.[5]

Why does rest show up in your rep count?

Resting between sets mostly matters because it decides whether you can repeat the work. Willardson's review makes the point directly: whether you keep a steady number of repetitions across consecutive sets depends largely on the rest interval.[7] The 2024 analysis suggests the small benefit of longer rests may be mediated by smaller drops in volume load.[5]

In our reading, this matters for pull-ups because your body weight is the load. You cannot take 5 percent off the bar when you are tired. The only thing that gives is your rep count. That is reasoning, not a pull-up finding, but it is why a rushed rest tends to show up as 6, 4, 3, 2 instead of 5, 5, 4, 4.

How long should you rest if you only manage a few reps?

Beginners are the group in which the review found 60 to 120 seconds sufficient for strength.[2] If you do one to three pull-ups or band-assisted reps per set, start near the top of that range, around 2 minutes, because grip, lats and shoulders still have to recover. Watch the reps instead of the clock: if the second set is clearly lower than the first, add 30 seconds next time. This is a practical suggestion, not a tested protocol.

Should you use a timer or rest by feel?

The research does not give a clear winner. The 2014 review found no evidence that predetermined rest periods beat auto-regulated ones for hypertrophy.[4] A workable middle path:

  1. Set a minimum with a timer, for example 90 seconds for volume work and 2 minutes for heavy work.
  2. After the minimum, start the next set when your breathing is calm and you expect to match your last set.
  3. Cap the rest at about 3 minutes so the session does not drag on.
  4. Log reps and rest time together, so you can see which rest lengths cost you reps.

What can a week with different rest times look like?

This is an example, not a tested program. With two pull-up sessions a week, use the longer rest on the day you aim for your hardest sets (for example heavy, low-rep or weighted sets, about 3 minutes) and a moderate rest on the volume day (for example 5 to 8 reps, about 90 seconds to 2 minutes). Keep the total number of hard sets similar from week to week, and change rest time or volume, not both at once. Which rest mistakes cost you reps?

  • Cutting rest to look intense, then losing half your reps by the third set.
  • Resting 5 minutes on every set, including easy volume sets, so the session eats your evening.
  • Changing rest, volume and grip in the same week, so you cannot tell what helped.
  • Judging a session by how winded you are instead of by clean, repeatable reps.
  • Skipping the rest when a joint is irritated. Rest is not a fix for pain; if a set hurts, stop it or change the exercise.

What can the research not tell you yet?

  • No pull-up trial. We found no study that compared rest times using pull-ups. The strength tests above used squat and bench press.
  • Who was studied. Mostly young men, many of them resistance-trained. Findings for women and older adults are thinner.
  • Short studies. Most trials ran a few weeks to a few months, and the muscle-size measurements are noisy.
  • Mixed results. The hypertrophy reviews describe large differences between studies, so a single range is a guide, not a promise.

Rest time is one lever among several. Total hard sets, how close to failure you train, sleep and consistency still decide most of your progress.

Frequently asked questions

How long should you rest between pull-up sets?

About 2 minutes is a sensible default, and up to 3 minutes for hard, low-rep sets. Reviews of resistance training support more than 2 minutes for maximal strength in trained people and 60 to 120 seconds for beginners, but none tested pull-ups specifically.

Can you rest 60 seconds and still get stronger?

Yes. A systematic review found robust strength gains even with rests under 60 seconds. For trained people, rests longer than 2 minutes seemed to maximise strength.

Do short rests build more muscle?

Not according to the research we found. A 2014 review and a 2024 meta-analysis found no advantage for short rests, and the 2024 analysis saw a small benefit for resting longer than 60 seconds with no clear gain beyond about 90 seconds.

Is it fine to rest by feel instead of the clock?

It looks fine. A 2014 review found no evidence that fixed rest periods beat resting by feel for muscle growth. A timer minimum plus a cap of about 3 minutes is a practical compromise.

How long should beginners rest between band-assisted or negative sets?

Start near 2 minutes. A systematic review found 60 to 120 seconds sufficient for strength in untrained people. If your reps fall sharply from set to set, add 30 seconds.
