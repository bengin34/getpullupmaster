Pull-ups for climbing: do they help, and how should you train them?

What the research shows, where it stops, and what to watch in your fingers·8 min read

For most climbers, pull-ups are a useful strength base: pulling strength is one of the things that separates climbers of different levels. But the evidence is correlational, a bar pull-up is not very climbing-specific, and it does nothing to protect your finger tendons. Use pull-ups to support climbing and finger training, not to replace them.

Do pull-ups help your climbing?

They can. A systematic review of 74 studies concluded that cardiorespiratory endurance and muscular strength, endurance and power are determinants of sport climbing performance, while balance, flexibility and body measurements seem to count less.[1] A mini-review of 25 studies on climbing tests describes maximal and explosive finger and upper-body strength as crucial for climbing performance, and adds that there is no agreement on how to assess that strength.[2]

The word "help" needs care. These studies compare climbers of different levels or relate a test to climbing grade. They show that stronger climbers tend to climb harder. They do not show that adding pull-ups to your week will raise your grade, and we did not find a controlled trial that tested exactly that for pull-ups.

What does the evidence say about pulling strength and climbing grade?

The most direct study we found measured an isometric pull-up on a 23 mm wooden rung in 78 male sport climbers, with a 90 degree elbow angle and a half-crimp grip. Peak force correlated strongly with climbing performance (Spearman's rho 0.73).[3] That is a pull on a climbing-style hold, not a bar pull-up, so it speaks to climbing strength more than to the exercise you do at the gym.

The pattern is consistent: more pulling force goes with higher grades. A correlation can run either way, though. Climbers who climb harder may also build more strength simply by climbing.

Why does a bar pull-up not transfer one to one?

On a bar you pull with both hands, a thick handle, a vertical body and a full range of motion. On a wall you often pull on small holds, with one arm doing more work than the other, with your feet on the wall and with the angle changing every move. The mini-review notes that dynamic upper-body tests such as pull-ups are reliable but could lack specificity compared with finger-focused tests.[2] The systematic review found that climbing-specific assessments separated climbers of different levels better than general functional tests.[1]

The practical reading is simple. Pull-ups build a general pulling base. The part of a pull that sits closest to climbing is the bent-arm position on a small hold, and that is where finger strength and lock-off strength meet.

Are pull-ups safe for your fingers and tendons?

Pull-ups on a bar are not the main finger risk in climbing. Small holds are. Up to 90 percent of climbers use the crimp grip, which puts the highest load on the A2 pulley, a band of tissue that holds the finger flexor tendon against the bone. In the crimp position the force on that pulley is roughly 3 to 4 times the force at the fingertip. Closed pulley ruptures are rare in the general population but much more common in climbers, usually appearing suddenly on a hard crimp move or after a shock load, with pain and swelling over the pulley and sometimes an audible pop.[4] One review states that around a third of all climbing injuries involve flexor tendons or their pulleys.[5]

Here is the link to pull-ups, and it is our reasoning, not a study result: a stronger back and arms let you pull harder on holds your fingers may not be ready for. Tendons do adapt to load, and the size of the load matters. A meta-analysis of exercise studies in healthy adults aged 18 to 50 found clear tendon adaptations, with loading magnitude as a key factor. Those studies looked at the Achilles and patellar tendons, not finger tendons, so we cannot say how fast your finger pulleys adapt.[6]

  • Add finger-loaded climbing and hanging gradually, and not in the same week you jump your pull-up load.
  • A sudden sharp pain at the base of a finger, a pop or swelling is a reason to stop and have it assessed. Milder pulley injuries (Schoffl grade 1 and 2) are usually managed without surgery, while a complete rupture (grade 4) is usually repaired.[5]
  • Elbow and shoulder pain are separate issues; our elbow tendon pain guide covers how to load them back in.

How should a climber train pull-ups?

No guideline exists for pull-up dosage in climbers. The general resistance-training position stand from the American College of Sports Medicine recommends loads of 8 to 12 repetition maximum for beginners, and for experienced lifters a wider range of 1 to 12 repetition maximum in a periodized plan with a heavy emphasis of 1 to 6 repetition maximum. It suggests 2 to 3 days a week for beginners and 3 to 4 for intermediates, and raising the load by 2 to 10 percent once you can do one or two extra reps.[7] That is general strength training, not climbing advice, but it is a sound frame. A reasonable way to apply it:

  • Do one or two pulling sessions a week next to your climbing, not on top of your hardest finger sessions.
  • Use strict reps: start from a full hang, chin over the bar, lower under control. If you cannot do one yet, start with our first pull-up guide.
  • Build reps first, then add weight. Once you can do clean sets, heavier low-rep sets develop strength with less fatigue than endless reps.
  • Stop a set while form is still clean. Tired, swinging reps teach you little and load the elbows.

Do lock-offs and weighted pull-ups make sense for climbers?

A lock-off is holding the bent-arm position with your chin or chest near the hands. The one study measuring strength closest to this used an elbow angle of 90 degrees and found it related to grade,[3] so a bar lock-off at about that angle matches what was tested. Whether training lock-offs raises climbing grade has not been tested in the studies we found, so treat it as a sensible option rather than a proven method.

Weighted pull-ups are the straightforward way to raise pulling strength once bodyweight reps are easy. The mini-review lists a one-repetition-maximum weighted pull-up as a reliable test (coefficient of variation 7.7 percent), with the caveat that it may lack climbing specificity.[2] Our weighted pull-up guide explains how to add load safely.

What does a simple week look like?

  1. Climb as normal. Your climbing sessions remain the main source of skill and finger strength.
  2. Add one or two pull-up sessions, ideally on a lighter climbing day or a rest day.
  3. Start with strict sets, then add short lock-off holds at the top, then add weight when sets feel clean.
  4. If your fingers, elbows or shoulders flare up, cut pulling volume first and see a clinician if pain persists.

Judge the plan by what happens on the wall: a hold you could not lock off before, a route finished with less sag at the crux. Our pull-up frequency guide helps with spacing your sessions.

Frequently asked questions

Do pull-ups help you climb harder?

Probably, as part of a wider plan. Studies link pulling strength with higher climbing grades, but they are correlational, and we did not find a controlled trial showing that pull-up training raises grade.

How many pull-ups do you need to climb a certain grade?

The reviews we read give no pull-up number tied to a grade. Pull-up counts appear as endurance tests, but without clear validity data, and they may lack specificity to climbing.

Can pull-ups hurt your fingers?

Bar pull-ups are not the main finger risk. Small-hold crimping is, because it loads the A2 pulley heavily. Stronger pulling can still let you load fingers harder than they are ready for, so progress finger work gradually.

Are lock-offs or weighted pull-ups better for climbers?

We found no study comparing them. A 90 degree isometric pull was linked with grade, which supports lock-offs, and weighted pull-ups are a reliable way to build general pulling strength. Many climbers use both.

Should you do pull-ups on climbing days?

No study answers this. A cautious approach is to keep heavy pulling and your hardest finger-loaded climbing on separate days where possible, and to cut pulling first if joints or fingers complain.
