Muscle-up progression: how to get your first muscle-up

Prerequisites, the transition and what the evidence says·8 min read

To get your first muscle-up, build strict pull-ups you can pull high toward your chest, add bar dips, then learn the transition over the bar with assisted and slow negative reps. Almost no research tests this route. The one study comparing bar and ring muscle-ups describes people who can already do them, so most of the advice below is coaching convention, and we label it as such.

What do you need before you try a muscle-up?

A muscle-up is a pull-up followed by a dip in one smooth movement. You pull until your chest is above the bar, turn your wrists and elbows over it, and press up until your arms are straight. That takes three things: enough pulling strength to get high above the bar, enough pressing strength to dip out of the bottom, and a transition that links the two.

No trial has tested which prerequisites predict a first muscle-up. The only study that compared bar and ring muscle-ups enrolled 10 recreationally active men with at least six months of high-intensity bodyweight training who could already do five muscle-ups on rings and on a bar.[1] It shows what the movement looks like in people who can do it, not how to get there. Everything below about getting there is coaching convention.

How strong do your pull-ups need to be?

The pull-up is the base. In an EMG study of 25 adults, the latissimus dorsi was the most active muscle in the pull-up, at 117 to 130 percent of a maximal voluntary contraction.[2] That fits the usual advice to build strict pull-ups first. If you cannot do one yet, start with our first pull-up guide.

Coaching guides often ask for around 10 strict pull-ups before muscle-up drills. That is a rule of thumb. We found no study that sets a threshold, so use it as a sanity check, not a pass mark. Our page on how many pull-ups is good gives standards by level, and weighted pull-ups are one way to build a reserve once bodyweight reps get easy.

For the muscle-up, height matters as much as reps. A standard pull-up ends with your chin over the bar. A muscle-up needs the bar to reach your lower chest or belly so your elbows can get over it. We found no study that measures how high people pull or how that relates to a first muscle-up. Coaches usually train it in three steps:

  • High pulls. Pull fast and aim the bar at your lower chest.
  • Chest-to-bar pull-ups. Touch the bar with your chest on every rep, with control.
  • Shoulder blades down. Start each rep from an active hang, not a sagging one.

Why is the transition the hard part?

The transition is the moment your chest passes the bar and your hands go from pulling below you to pressing under you. Coaches describe it as the point where most attempts stall. We found no study that isolates it, so this is experience, not evidence.

The research that exists splits the movement in two. In the bar-versus-ring study, each muscle-up was divided into a pull phase and a push phase. The biceps and the forearm flexors were more active in the pull phase than in the push phase.[1] That matches what you feel: the first half is a hard pull, the second half a press.

Most coaching advice treats the transition as a position and timing problem more than a strength problem. Common cues, none of them tested:

  • Keep the bar close to your body as you pull.
  • Lean your chest forward over the bar and bring both elbows up together.
  • Do not stop between the pull and the press.

Does a kip help or get in the way?

Most muscle-ups use a small swing. A study of 11 athletes compared 5 standard and 5 kipping pull-ups. The kip added about 49 degrees of hip and 57 degrees of knee motion, raised rectus abdominis activity by about 29 percentage points of maximal contraction and iliopsoas by about 26, and lowered biceps activity by about 27 points. The authors note that athletes may manage more reps because their upper body tires less.[3]

That is a pull-up kip, not a muscle-up kip, and we found no study that measures the muscle-up kip itself. It does suggest why a swing can make a first rep easier and why it is not the same as upper-body strength. If you want the trade-offs, read kipping vs strict pull-ups. The bar-versus-ring study allowed a kip and did not test strict muscle-ups, which limits what it says about strength.[1]

Do you need dips before a muscle-up?

Yes, in practice. The second half of a muscle-up is a press, and a dip is the simplest way to train it. In a study of 13 experienced men, the mean peak triceps activation was 0.83 mV on the bench dip, 1.04 on the bar dip and 1.05 on the ring dip. The authors call the bar dip an appropriate progression from the bench dip because of its higher peak activation.[4]

A second study had 15 men do bar dips to exhaustion. Pectoralis major and triceps activation rose as they tired, while peak joint angles did not change.[5] So a set of dips taken close to failure does load the chest and triceps, the muscles that finish a muscle-up.

Coaching guides often ask for roughly 10 to 15 clean bar dips, but we found no study that checks that number. Also, both studies used parallel bars, while the muscle-up press happens over a single bar. No one has measured the difference.

Should you learn it on a bar or on rings?

Start on the bar. Of eight muscles recorded, only four differed between ring and bar muscle-ups, and all were higher on rings: the upper trapezius and forearm flexors in the pull phase, the triceps in the push phase, and the biceps overall. Lats, lower trapezius, serratus anterior and pectoralis major did not differ significantly.[1]

The authors suggest the bar may be easier to learn first, and that rings need good upper-limb strength. Keep the caveats in mind. Only 10 men took part, below the 12 the authors planned for, everyone could already do the movement, and a kip was allowed.

What does a sensible muscle-up progression look like?

This is a practical suggestion, not a tested protocol. Move on when the current step feels controlled, not when you have just scraped it.

  1. Strict pull-ups with a full hang each rep. Build volume first; the pull-up workout plan shows a weekly structure.
  2. High pulls and chest-to-bar pull-ups, so the bar reaches your lower chest.
  3. Bar dips, and straight bar dips if your station allows them.
  4. Slow negatives from the top of the muscle-up position, lowering over the bar.
  5. Assisted muscle-ups: a band, a low bar with a small jump, or a spotter.
  6. Full muscle-ups, first with a small kip, then work toward a stricter rep.

Two sessions a week of muscle-up work, after a warm-up, are plenty while you keep your regular pull-up training. If you stall on one step for weeks, see our pull-up plateau page.

What can go wrong?

The research here is case reports, which show that something can happen but not how often. One describes a 30-year-old CrossFit athlete who felt sudden pain in the back of the shoulder during a bar muscle-up. MRI showed a torn latissimus dorsi tendon, which was repaired surgically. He reported about 90 percent shoulder function after nine months.[6] Another describes a 23-year-old woman with a complete pectoralis major tear after a muscle-up, who returned to high-intensity interval training 11 months after surgery.[7]

We found no injury-rate data for muscle-ups. Sensible precautions: warm up the shoulders, avoid big explosive attempts when tired, and stop at a sharp pain in the shoulder or chest instead of pushing through.

What does the evidence not tell us?

  • Which pull-up or dip numbers predict a first muscle-up.
  • Whether chest-to-bar pulls or high pulls are the best preparation.
  • How the transition works, because it has not been isolated in a study.
  • Whether the muscle-up kip behaves like the pull-up kip.
  • How often muscle-ups cause injuries, and in whom.
  • How women and beginners respond, since the muscle-up study enrolled only men who could already do it.

Frequently asked questions

How many pull-ups do you need before a muscle-up?

No study sets a number. Coaching guides often quote around 10 strict pull-ups, which is a rule of thumb. What matters more is pulling high enough that the bar reaches your lower chest.

Are dips required for a muscle-up?

In practice yes, because the second half is a press. Bar dips load the triceps and pectoralis major, and coaching guides often suggest roughly 10 to 15 clean reps, though no study tests that figure.

Is the bar or the rings muscle-up easier?

Authors of the only comparison suggest the bar may be easier to learn first. On rings, the biceps, triceps, upper trapezius and forearm flexors were more active in a study of 10 men who could already do both.

Can you learn a muscle-up with a kip?

Most people do. A kip adds hip and knee motion and reduces biceps activity in a pull-up study, so it can make a rep easier. We found no study of the muscle-up kip itself.

How long does it take to get a first muscle-up?

We found no data. It depends on your starting pull-up and dip strength and on how often you practice the transition, so track steps rather than weeks.
