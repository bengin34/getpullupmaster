One-arm pull-up progression: prerequisites, steps and a realistic timeline

A practitioner roadmap, with the evidence gaps spelled out·9 min read

A one-arm pull-up asks one arm and one shoulder to lift your whole body, and most people need many months of heavy two-arm work before they can even attempt the first step. The usual route is strong weighted pull-ups, then archer and typewriter pull-ups, then one-arm negatives and assisted one-arm reps. That route comes from coaching practice, not from trials, and this page says so wherever it matters.

Is the one-arm pull-up realistic for you?

In a normal pull-up, two arms share your body weight. In a one-arm pull-up, one arm carries all of it, so the working side faces roughly twice the load, plus a twisting pull because the body wants to rotate around the single hand. For a sense of scale, a study of 25 young adults measured muscle activity in a two-arm pull-up and found the latissimus dorsi reached 117 to 130 percent of its maximum voluntary isometric effort, with the biceps at 78 to 96 percent.[1] Those numbers come from two-arm reps. Removing one arm does not halve the demand on the remaining muscles, it pushes them toward their ceiling.

That is why the skill is rare, and why it is fair to ask first whether you want the months or years of work. If your goal is strong, muscular pulling, you can get there without ever doing one. If the one-arm pull-up itself is the goal, read on.

Which prerequisites should you have first?

No study defines a threshold for starting one-arm work. The benchmarks below are common coaching rules of thumb, and you should read them as a sensible filter, not a tested cut-off.

PrerequisiteTypical benchmarkWhy it matters
Strict bodyweight pull-ups12 or more clean reps, chin clearly over the barShows a solid base of pulling strength and shoulder control
Weighted pull-upAround 50 to 75 percent of body weight added for a few repsTeaches your body to handle loads above your own weight
One-arm dead hang20 to 30 seconds per sideGrip, shoulder and elbow tolerance for hanging from one hand
Joint healthNo ongoing elbow or shoulder painThe next steps load the tendons far more than normal pull-ups

If you are not there yet, build the base first with a weighted pull-up progression. Heavy loading is where progression is best supported: the American College of Sports Medicine recommends that advanced lifters eventually emphasise heavy loads of 1 to 6 repetition maximum with 3 to 5 minutes of rest, and that exercise selection include both bilateral and unilateral movements.[2] That position stand covers healthy adults in general, not this lift.

What are the steps of the progression?

Practitioners usually order the work like this. Move on only when the current step feels controlled for every rep.

  1. Heavy weighted pull-ups. Two arms, extra load, strict form. This is the foundation, and you can keep training it through every later step.
  2. Archer pull-ups. Use a wide grip. Pull toward one hand while the other arm stays nearly straight and acts as a support. Start with the straight arm doing a lot of the work, then let it do less over time.
  3. Typewriter pull-ups. Pull up to one side, slide across the top toward the other hand, and go back down. This trains the top position under load, where the one-arm rep is hardest to hold.
  4. Assisted one-arm pull-ups. Hold the bar with one hand and grip a towel, band or the wrist of the working arm with the other. Over weeks, reduce how hard the helper hand pulls. A fingertip grip on the wrist is a late stage.
  5. One-arm negatives. Reach the top with help, release the helper, and lower on one arm for three to five seconds. Our negative pull-up guide explains the lowering technique with two arms first.
  6. One-arm lock-offs. Hold the top or mid-range position on one arm for a few seconds. This builds the strength to stop and own the position.
  7. Full one-arm pull-up. Start with a single clean rep per side after a rest, not at the end of a hard session.

You can use a resistance band in step four by looping it around the bar and your forearm or hand, and then moving to a thinner band as you improve.

How should you dose the training?

There is no tested protocol, so the framework below applies general progressive overload and keeps the volume low on purpose. The ACSM recommends increasing load by 2 to 10 percent once you can do one or two repetitions more than your target, and that fits the idea of making small changes.[2]

PhaseMain workSets x repsDays per week
BaseWeighted pull-ups4-5 x 3-52
Unilateral biasArcher or typewriter3-4 x 3-5 per side2
One-arm specificAssisted reps and negatives3-5 x 1-3 per side1-2
Lock-offsOne-arm holds3 x 3-5 s per side1-2

Rest three to five minutes between heavy sets, and leave at least one full day between sessions that load the same elbow. Do both sides every time, but begin with your weaker arm. Keep two or three clean reps in reserve for the first few weeks instead of training to failure. Our pull-up plateau guide covers what to change when progress stalls.

How long does it take?

Nobody has measured it, so every timeline is an estimate based on what coaches and athletes report. It depends heavily on your body weight, your training history and how well your tendons cope with the load. A lighter person who already does weighted pull-ups will move faster than a heavier one who is working toward the benchmarks.

Where you startRough practitioner estimate
Under 10 strict pull-upsBuild the base first, often a year or more before one-arm work starts
12+ pull-ups, little weighted workSeveral months of weighted training, then 6 to 12 months of one-arm specific steps
Solid weighted pull-upsRoughly 6 to 12 months of one-arm specific steps

Treat these as ranges, not promises. Some people take two years or more and some never get a clean rep with their heavier arm. Tendons are one reason: a meta-analysis of exercise studies found that tendon stiffness adapts to loading and that the magnitude of the load plays the key role, with interventions lasting at least eight weeks, and it looked at the Achilles and patellar tendons, not the elbow.[3] Tendons take weeks to months to adapt, so rushing the steps is the main way to lose time.

How do you protect your elbows and shoulders?

This is the part that deserves the most attention. One-arm work puts roughly double the load through one elbow flexor tendon and one shoulder, and the speed at which you add that load is up to you.

  • Elbows. Load-induced tendinopathy shows up with different levels of pain, irritability and capacity, and some tendons recover with simple measures while others resist treatment.[4] Because the course is unpredictable, do not ignore a sore elbow that keeps returning. Read our elbow tendon pain guide.
  • Shoulders. A review of resistance-training injuries found that up to 36 percent happened at the shoulder, linked to joint and muscle imbalances and poor exercise technique.[5] A biomechanics study found that high arm elevation during pull-ups narrows the sub-acromial space and raises impingement risk, mainly for wide and reversed grips.[6] A one-arm pull-up was not tested there, so use the finding as a reason for care, and keep the shoulder blade controlled. See our rotator cuff guide.
  • Warning signs. Sharp pain, pinching in the front of the shoulder, tingling, or elbow pain that is still there the next morning all mean you should reduce load or drop back a step.
  • Pace. Add one new variable at a time, keep the weekly volume low, and take a lighter week every four to six weeks.

If pain lasts more than a couple of weeks or you notice weakness, see a physiotherapist or physician who works with lifters.

What mistakes slow you down?

  • Jumping to the one-arm hang too early. Skipping the weighted base is the fastest way to an overloaded elbow.
  • Using the helper hand as the main puller. If the free hand does more than half of the pull, the step is not doing what you need.
  • Swinging and kipping. Momentum hides the weakness you are trying to train.
  • Training the stronger arm more. Match the sets on both sides and start with the weaker arm.
  • Training heavy every day. Heavy tendon loading needs recovery days.

What does the research not show?

No controlled study has tested a training programme for the one-arm pull-up, so we cannot say which steps work best, how fast people progress, or what benchmarks predict success. The progression above, including the prerequisites, the order of archers and typewriters, the dosing table and the timelines, is practitioner consensus. The sources on this page describe muscle activity in two-arm pull-ups, general resistance-training principles, tendon adaptation in other tendons and injury patterns, and none of them tested this lift. Treat the page as a cautious starting framework and adjust it to how your body responds.

How does it fit into a normal pull-up plan?

Most people do best when one-arm work sits on top of a regular plan, not instead of it. Keep a heavy two-arm day, add one unilateral day, and keep the rest of your week for easier volume. If you are still working on your first rep, ignore everything above and start with a plan built for that stage.

Frequently asked questions

How long does it take to learn a one-arm pull-up?

Nobody has measured it. Coaches commonly report 6 to 12 months of one-arm specific work after a solid weighted base, and some people need two years or more. Body weight and tendon tolerance change the estimate a lot.

How many pull-ups do I need before starting?

A common rule of thumb is 12 strict reps and a weighted pull-up with roughly half your body weight added. It is a coaching benchmark, not a tested threshold, so use it as a filter and listen to your elbows.

Are archer or typewriter pull-ups better?

They train different things. Archers shift load toward one arm, and typewriters train the top position under load. Most practitioners use both, and no study compares them for one-arm progress.

Can I get a one-arm pull-up by using a band?

A band can assist the one-arm rep and let you practise the movement with less load. Use thinner bands over time, and pair the practice with heavy weighted pull-ups so strength keeps building.

Is the one-arm pull-up bad for my elbows and shoulders?

It loads one elbow and one shoulder heavily, so the risk of overload is real if you rush. Increase load slowly, keep volume low and stop at sharp or lingering pain. See a clinician if it persists.
