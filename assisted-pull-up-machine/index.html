Assisted pull-up machine: how to use it and how to wean off

Counterweight, machine vs band, and a plan to need less help every week·7 min read

The assisted pull-up machine subtracts weight from your body with a counterweight stack, so you can practise the full pull-up movement before you can do it unassisted. Pick an assistance that lets you finish 8 to 12 clean reps, then reduce it in small steps once the top of that range feels easy. It is a useful tool, but no trial has shown that it gets you to a first pull-up faster than bands or negatives.

What does the assisted pull-up machine actually do?

You kneel on a pad or stand on a platform, grab the handles and pull your chest toward them. The platform is connected to a weight stack. The more weight you select, the more the platform pushes you up, and the less of your body weight your arms have to lift. Select 30 kg on the stack and, as a rough rule of thumb, you lift your body weight minus about 30 kg. Machines differ in pad geometry and friction, so treat the number on the stack as an approximation and not as a precise load.

That is the whole appeal. You pull yourself, not a handle toward you, through the same range as a pull-up, and you can change the help in small, repeatable steps. Compare this with a lat pulldown, where you move a bar while your body stays seated; our lat pulldown vs pull-up guide explains why those are different tasks. One study of chin-ups against lat pulldowns found different biceps and erector spinae activity and different shoulder and neck movement between the two.[3] That study did not test an assisted machine, so the sensible reading is only this: a movement where you carry your own body is not the same as one where you pull a bar down.

How do you choose the counterweight?

Start with more help than your ego wants. A practical target is an assistance level at which you can complete three sets of 8 to 12 reps with the same form on every rep. The American College of Sports Medicine recommends loads that correspond to an 8 to 12 repetition maximum for novices, and training two to three days a week.[1] Use that as your frame, not as a promise that it works for pull-ups specifically.

A simple way to find the number:

  1. Guess high. Choose an assistance of roughly half your body weight.
  2. Do one set to near failure. If you get more than 12 clean reps, remove help. If you get fewer than 8, add help.
  3. Adjust by one or two plates. Write the number down so next week starts where you finished.
  4. Check the range. Your arms should start straight and your chin should end over the handles. Reps that stop halfway do not count, whatever the stack says.

The same ACSM position stand suggests increasing load by 2 to 10 percent once you can do one to two reps over your target.[1] The stack makes this harder than it sounds. If you weigh 80 kg and use 40 kg of help, you are lifting about 40 kg. Removing one 5 kg plate raises that load by about 12 percent, which is already above the range. That is why extra reps, a slower lowering phase or half-plates (if your machine has them) are better first moves than a big drop in assistance.

Machine or band: which one is better?

They help in different ways, and the difference is mechanical. A machine gives the same assistance through the whole range. A band stretches, so it pushes hardest at the bottom, where it is most stretched, and gives less help near the top. Our band-assisted pull-ups guide covers how to set up a band.

If you train in a gym, the machine is the more precise tool because you know your starting weight and you can record it. If you train at home, the band is the practical one. Which of the two builds a first pull-up faster is not known, as the evidence section below explains.

What does the research say about muscle use?

A study of 19 strength-trained men compared pull-up grips and found that the shoulder, arm and forearm muscles were similar across grips over a full rep. It also found that the biceps, brachioradialis and pectoralis major were more active in the pulling phase than in the lowering phase.[2] That study used unassisted pull-ups, so it says nothing directly about the machine. What it does suggest is that pulling and lowering are different jobs for your muscles, and a plan should include both.

Lowering is also where you are strongest. A review of eccentric muscle work reports that greater force is produced during eccentric contractions than during other contraction types at the same speed.[4] On the machine you can use this: pull up with a higher assistance and then lower slowly. Treat that as a coaching idea, as no trial has tested it on this machine.

How do you wean off the machine?

The goal is to need less stack every week, not to hit a number on a calendar. A cautious pattern looks like this.

  1. Add reps at a fixed assistance until you hit 12 clean reps in all three sets.
  2. Cut assistance by one step and drop back to about 8 reps. Build up again.
  3. Add a slow lowering phase. Pull up, then take three to five seconds to come down. This is the machine version of a negative pull-up.
  4. Test one unassisted attempt every two weeks. Do it first in the session, when you are fresh, not after your sets.
  5. Mix in other tools once assistance is down to about a quarter of your body weight: bands, negatives and hangs.

If you stall for two weeks, do not skip ahead. Return to the last assistance that felt clean and add reps. Two or three sessions a week is the frequency the ACSM stand gives for novices.[1] Our first pull-up guide shows how the machine fits among hangs, rows and negatives.

What mistakes hold people back?

  • Too much help for too long. If every set is 15 easy reps at the same weight, the stack is doing the work.
  • Pushing off the pad. Driving with your legs removes load from your arms and inflates the number.
  • Half reps. Stopping with bent elbows at the bottom or with the chin under the handles trains a smaller range.
  • Dropping on the way down. Falling back to the start skips the lowering strength the machine can give you.
  • Shrugging and swinging. Start each rep by pulling your shoulder blades down, then bend your elbows. See our pull-up mistakes guide for form fixes.
  • Comparing stack numbers between gyms. Machines from different brands do not use the same scale or geometry, so track reps and form, not just kilograms.

What does research not show?

We found no trial that compares machine-assisted pull-ups, band-assisted pull-ups and negatives for getting a first pull-up. That means nobody can tell you from data which tool is fastest, how large each step down should be, or how many weeks the transition takes. The studies cited here are about muscle activity in unassisted pull-ups, general strength training guidelines and eccentric work, and none of them tested the assisted machine on beginners. The step sizes and the 8-week example above are coaching conventions, so adjust them to how you feel and recover.

Frequently asked questions

How much assistance should I use on the assisted pull-up machine?

Pick the weight at which you can do 8 to 12 clean, full-range reps for three sets. A starting guess of about half your body weight is common. Adjust after your first set and record the number.

Is the assisted pull-up machine better than bands?

Neither has been shown to be better for a first pull-up. The machine gives steady, exact assistance, and a band gives more help at the bottom and is portable. Choose the one you can use consistently.

How fast should I reduce the weight stack?

Slowly. Add reps at the same weight first, then lower the assistance by one step once you reach 12 reps in every set. Expect to drop back to about 8 reps after each step.

Does the assisted pull-up machine build the same muscles as a real pull-up?

It trains a similar movement, since you move your own body through the range. The research on muscle activity used unassisted pull-ups, so we cannot say how close the machine is.

When should I stop using the machine?

When assistance is a small fraction of your body weight, try unassisted attempts at the start of your session. Once you can do a clean rep, keep using negatives or bands for extra reps.
