How long does it take to do a pull-up?

An honest timeline from where you are today to your first strict rep·8 min read

It depends mostly on where you start: someone who can already hang and lower slowly may be a few weeks away, while someone who cannot hang for 10 seconds is usually looking at a few months. We found no trial that timed this, so every range below is a rough coaching guideline, not a measured result. Use it to plan, then let your own logs correct it.

How long does it take to do your first pull-up?

There is no single number, and anyone who gives you one is guessing. No study we could find followed adults from zero to a first strict pull-up and recorded the weeks. What we have instead is general training science on how strength responds to regular, progressive practice[1], plus a lot of coaching experience about how beginners tend to move through the stages.

The useful way to think about it is stages, not a calendar date. You are not waiting for a day to arrive. You are moving through a short list of abilities: hanging from the bar, holding the top, lowering under control and finally pulling up. Each stage has its own rough duration, and your timeline is the sum of the stages you still have to cover.

What decides your timeline?

  • Your starting point. This is the biggest factor. A person who can lower slowly from the top already owns the hardest half of the movement. A person who cannot yet hang for a few seconds has more stages to cover.
  • Body weight. A pull-up lifts your whole body, so extra weight is extra load on the same muscles. This is not a verdict on you, only on the arithmetic. Our body weight and pull-ups guide covers it in detail.
  • How often you train. Beginners usually do well with two to three pulling sessions a week, spaced so you recover between them[1]. More is not automatically better; see how often to do pull-ups.
  • Weekly volume. In a meta-analysis, more weekly sets per muscle group were associated with larger muscle gains, with returns that shrink as volume climbs[2]. For a beginner that means steady, repeated work beats one heroic session.
  • Consistency over months. Skipped weeks reset more than they save. A plan you can keep for 12 weeks beats a harder plan you drop at week three.
  • Age, injury history and sleep. These change how fast you recover, not whether you can get there. If something hurts, fix that first.

Where are you starting? A five-minute self-test

Do these in order and stop at the first one you cannot finish. Rest a couple of minutes between them and warm up your shoulders first.

  1. Dead hang. Hang from the bar with straight arms and your feet off the floor. Can you hold 20 seconds?
  2. Scapular pull. From the hang, pull your shoulder blades down and back without bending your elbows. Can you do five slow reps? See scapular pull-ups.
  3. Top hold. Step or jump to the top position, chin over the bar. Can you hold it for five seconds?
  4. Negative. From the top, lower for a slow count of five. Can you do three of those? See negative pull-ups.
  5. Row. Do a bodyweight row under a low bar. Can you do eight with your body in a straight line?

The step where you stop is your level. Write it down with the date. It is the baseline the rest of this page refers to.

What does a realistic timeline look like?

The ranges below are rough coaching guidelines for someone training two to three times a week and recovering well. They are not trial data. Some people will be faster, plenty will be slower, and a month of illness or travel adds to any of them.

Notice what the table does not say: it gives no promise. If your logs show steady progress, you are on track even when you sit at the slow end of a range. If nothing has moved for four weeks, change something, as covered in the pull-up plateau guide.

What speeds it up?

  • Practise the movement itself. A study of climbers found that pull-up performance responded differently depending on the type of contraction they trained[5]. The takeaway for a beginner is to include actual pulling and lowering, not only general exercises.
  • Add reps before adding difficulty. A trial comparing load progression with repetition progression found similar muscle adaptations, so adding a rep to a set is a legitimate way to progress[4].
  • Rest enough between hard sets. In trained men, resting three minutes between sets produced larger strength gains than one minute[3]. Beginners are not the same population, but cutting rest to rush a hard pulling set rarely helps. See rest time between sets.
  • Stop before failure on most sets. Stopping a rep or two short keeps form clean and lets you train again in two days.
  • Sleep and enough food. We found no pull-up-specific trial on these, so treat them as general recovery basics rather than a hack.

What slows it down?

  • Training to exhaustion every session, which pushes your next session back. See training to failure.
  • Only doing the easiest variation forever. If you have done the same band or jump for six weeks, shrink the help.
  • Sloppy hangs with shrugged shoulders and bent elbows. They feel easy and teach little.
  • Changing the plan every week. You cannot see progress if the test keeps moving.
  • Pushing through elbow or shoulder pain. A week off costs less than a month of tendon trouble.

A simple way to track the weeks

Retest the self-test every two weeks, not every session. Record the best number from each step: hang seconds, scapular reps, top-hold seconds, negative count, row reps. A rising number in any step counts as progress, even when your first pull-up still feels far away.

That is also the logic behind the PullUp Master app: it places you on a progression block, guides every set and rest, and estimates how many weeks your first rep is away based on your logged sessions. An estimate like that is only as good as the data behind it, so treat it as a moving forecast, not a promise.

What should you do after the first rep?

Do not test it every day. One clean rep is a milestone, not a plan. For the next few weeks, keep the stages that got you there, add a second and third rep gradually, and use easy sets with a few reps left in reserve. When you can do three reps in a row, the pull-up workout plan is the natural next step, and our first pull-up guide covers the movement in full.

If you are not there yet, that is fine. Pick the step from the self-test where you stopped, train it twice this week and write down the result. That is how every first pull-up begins.

Frequently asked questions

How long does it take to do your first pull-up?

It depends on your starting level. Roughly, a few weeks if you can already lower slowly from the top, one to three months if you can hang but not lower, and often three to six months if you cannot yet hang for 10 seconds. These are coaching guidelines, not trial results.

Can you get a pull-up in 30 days?

Sometimes, if you start with good negatives and top holds. For most beginners 30 days is too short, and chasing it can cost you elbow or shoulder trouble. Aim for steady stage-by-stage progress instead.

How many times a week should I train to get a pull-up?

Two to three pulling sessions a week with a day or more between them suits most beginners, in line with general progression guidance for novices.

Why am I not getting better?

The usual causes are training to failure every time, changing the plan too often, or staying on the same assisted variation. Retest every two weeks and change one thing at a time.

Does body weight change how long it takes?

Yes, because you are lifting your own body. Heavier people can still get there; the path may simply start with rows and hangs and take longer.
