Sore after pull-ups: how long it lasts, whether to train and when to worry

Delayed-onset soreness in lats, biceps and forearms, and how to tell it from pain·8 min read

Soreness in your lats, biceps and forearms a day or two after pull-ups is almost always delayed-onset muscle soreness (DOMS). It usually peaks between 24 and 72 hours and fades within about a week. You can often train again lightly or work other muscles, but sharp pain in a joint or tendon, or dark urine, is a different story.

Why do pull-ups make you sore?

Delayed-onset muscle soreness is a dull, aching pain that you feel when you press on, contract or stretch the affected muscle. It typically appears 12 to 24 hours after unaccustomed eccentric exercise, peaks between 24 and 72 hours, and then fades and disappears within 5 to 7 days.[1] Eccentric work means the muscle produces force while it lengthens, and that is exactly what happens when you lower yourself from the bar.

A review of treatment strategies notes that eccentric activities cause micro-injury more often and more severely than other types of muscle action. It also says DOMS is most common when you return after a break or start a new kind of training.[2] Think of your first week of negatives, a jump in weekly sets or a new variation such as chin-ups.

Why the soreness happens is still not settled. The same review lists six proposed mechanisms, from muscle damage to inflammation, and concludes that a mix of two or more probably explains it.[2] Soreness is not a precise gauge either: DOMS intensity correlates poorly with other markers of muscle damage.[1]

Pull-ups load the back, the arm and the hands at once. This table is a practical guide, not a finding from a pull-up soreness study.

How long does soreness after pull-ups last?

Expect a mild day one, the worst on day two, easing by day three or four, and gone within the week.[1] Those numbers come from eccentric exercise research in general, not from pull-ups, so treat them as a typical range and not a promise.

Two things shift the timeline: how new the stimulus is, and how much lowering work you did. Slow negatives or a big jump in total reps are more likely to leave you sore for longer than crisp, easy sets. Soreness that is still getting worse after day four, or that has not gone after about a week, is a reason to look for another cause.

Can you train pull-ups while sore?

Often yes, with a lower dose. The treatment review reports that exercise is the most effective way to ease the pain of DOMS, although the relief is also temporary. It also reports that DOMS can reduce joint range of motion and peak torque, and that altered muscle recruitment can put unusual stress on ligaments and tendons, which may raise the risk of further injury if you return to full intensity too early.[2]

The authors' practical advice for athletes who train every day is to cut the intensity and duration for one to two days after DOMS-inducing exercise, or to train less affected body parts while the sore muscles recover.[2] For pull-ups, that suggests a few options:

  • Mild soreness: warm up well, do your normal session with a set or two fewer, and stop each set with reps left in the tank.
  • Moderate soreness: swap in lighter work such as easy sets, rows or leg and core training, and leave negatives and max sets for later. Our negative pull-up guide covers how to dose the lowering phase.
  • Severe soreness, or a clearly weaker pull: take the day off.

The right gap between sessions is a separate question that depends on your volume. See how often to do pull-ups and how to warm up your shoulders first.

Do massage, cold or stretching help?

A little, in some cases. A systematic review and meta-analysis of 99 studies found that active recovery, massage, compression garments, immersion, contrast water therapy and cryotherapy produced a small to large decrease in DOMS, with massage the most effective method for DOMS and perceived fatigue. Other methods in the analysis showed no change.[3] The older treatment review reached a more mixed verdict: massage gave varying results depending on timing and technique, anti-inflammatory drugs showed dose-dependent effects, and cryotherapy, stretching, ultrasound and electrical current showed no effect on soreness.[2]

The two reviews disagree on cold, so hold the claims loosely. The effects ranged widely, and they concern how sore you feel, not how fast the muscle rebuilds. If a gentle massage or an easy walk feels good, do it, but there is no strong reason to buy equipment for it.

How do you get less sore next time?

Your muscles adapt quickly. After a first bout of damaging eccentric exercise, a repeat bout typically brings attenuated soreness and faster recovery of strength and range of motion. This is called the repeated bout effect.[1] The protective effect appears after a single session, but the adaptation looks more complete after several.[1] The mechanisms are only partly understood; researchers propose that neural changes, muscle mechanics, connective tissue remodelling and biochemical signalling all contribute.[4]

The practical rule follows directly. The treatment review recommends introducing eccentric exercise or a novel activity progressively over one to two weeks.[2] For pull-ups:

  1. Start new variations with a small dose, for example two or three sets of a few reps.
  2. Add volume in small steps rather than doubling your weekly sets in one week.
  3. Repeat the same session once before you make it harder, so the adaptation can work for you.

Soreness or pain: what are the red flags?

DOMS is dull, widespread within the muscle, and tender to touch or stretch.[1] Pain that does not fit that description deserves attention before your next session.

  • Sharp or pinpoint pain at the elbow, especially on the inner or outer bony bump or near the biceps tendon, is not typical soreness. Read our elbow tendon pain guide.
  • Pain at the front or top of the shoulder, pain that catches at a certain angle, or pain when you lift the arm overhead belongs on the rotator cuff page.
  • Pain that is present at rest, wakes you at night or keeps getting worse instead of easing by day four or five.
  • Numbness, tingling or a sudden loss of strength in the arm or hand.
  • Dark urine with muscle soreness or cramps after very hard exercise.

The last item matters because exertional rhabdomyolysis, a breakdown of muscle cells after intense exercise that can lead to kidney problems, is described as underestimated. A systematic review of 772 athletes, most of them runners, with weightlifters the second biggest group, concluded that people with muscle soreness or cramps and dark urine after heavy endurance events should be screened.[5] That review covers athletes after extreme efforts, not typical pull-up sessions, and soreness alone is not a sign of it. If you notice dark urine together with soreness, see a doctor. The other items above are practical warning signs, not a diagnosis; when in doubt, ask a clinician.

What does the research not show?

We found no trials on DOMS after pull-ups. We also found no study that tested how to train pull-ups while sore, or how long to wait before the next session. The timeline, the advice to reduce training for one to two days and the repeated bout effect all come from research on eccentric exercise in general, often on the elbow flexors or the legs, not on pull-ups.

The remedy evidence is thin for the same reason. Reviews show effects of massage, cold and compression on how sore people feel, with a wide range and some disagreement between reviews, and none of them tested pull-up training. Use the advice on this page as cautious coaching, not as a tested protocol.

Frequently asked questions

How long does soreness after pull-ups last?

Typically it starts 12 to 24 hours after the session, peaks between 24 and 72 hours and is gone within 5 to 7 days. That timeline comes from eccentric exercise research in general, not from pull-up studies.

Is it OK to do pull-ups when my lats are sore?

Often yes, with less volume and lighter effort. A review of DOMS suggests cutting intensity and duration for one to two days or training less affected body parts. Skip max sets if your pull feels clearly weaker.

Does soreness mean the workout was effective?

Not necessarily. DOMS intensity correlates poorly with other markers of muscle damage, so a sore day does not prove a good session, and no soreness does not prove a bad one.

Do ice baths or massage speed up recovery from pull-up soreness?

They may lower how sore you feel a little. A 99-study meta-analysis found massage was the most effective method, but reviews disagree on cold, and no study tested pull-ups.

When is pain after pull-ups not normal soreness?

Sharp or pinpoint pain at the elbow or shoulder, numbness, pain at rest or soreness that keeps getting worse after day four call for a closer look. Dark urine with soreness after very hard exercise needs a doctor.
