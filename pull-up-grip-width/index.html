Pull-up grip width: wide, shoulder-width, narrow or neutral?

What the studies show and how to choose·8 min read

For most people a grip about shoulder width or slightly wider, overhand or neutral, is a sound default. Studies find broadly similar muscle activation across widths and hand positions, though the samples are small and the results do not always agree. The clearest warning concerns very wide grips, which put the shoulder in a more demanding position.

What does your grip change in a pull-up?

Two things change when you move your hands: where the arms start relative to the body (width) and how the forearm is turned (overhand, underhand or neutral). They act on different joints, so it helps to keep them apart.

Width acts at the shoulder. The latissimus dorsi arises from the lower thoracic spinous processes, the thoracolumbar fascia, the back of the iliac crest, the lower ribs and the tip of the shoulder blade. It inserts on the floor of the intertubercular groove of the upper arm bone and extends, adducts and internally rotates the arm.[1] With a narrow grip the arms start closer to the front of your body. With a wide grip they start out to the sides, with the shoulder more abducted, and your elbows have to travel sideways to reach your ribs.

Orientation acts at the elbow and forearm. The biceps brachii inserts on the radial tuberosity of the forearm, so it flexes the elbow and also turns the palm up (supination).[2] That is why turning your palms toward you changes how much the biceps contributes.

Overhand, underhand or neutral: what do the studies show?

An EMG study of 25 adults compared a pull-up, a chin-up and a pull-up on rotating handles. The lats were the most active muscle in all three, at 117 to 130 percent of a maximal voluntary contraction. The pectoralis major and biceps were more active in the chin-up, the lower trapezius more active in the pull-up, and the rotating handles made no difference compared with either.[3]

A second study of 19 strength-trained men tested underhand, overhand, neutral and rope pull-ups across eight arm and shoulder muscles. Over a full repetition the only reported difference was the middle trapezius, which was more active overhand than neutral (peak 60.1 versus 37.1 percent of maximum).[4] The authors concluded that activation is similar across hand orientations.

Does a wider grip build more lats?

The popular belief is that wider hands mean wider lats. The direct evidence is thinner than the belief.

A 2026 study of 21 men varied grip width and hand angle across ten pull-up conditions. A wide hand position combined with a pronounced angle raised activation of the pectoralis major and rectus abdominis by 14 and 15 percentage points of maximal contraction. The 0 degree angle gave higher activation of the infraspinatus, lower trapezius and lats, up to 60 percent of maximum. The abstract does not define the angle further and does not report a lat advantage for the wide position.[5]

In the lat pull-down, which is a related but different exercise, 15 men used grips of one, one and a half and two times their shoulder-bone (biacromial) distance. Lat, trapezius and infraspinatus activation was similar across widths over the whole movement. The biceps was more active with the medium grip than the narrow one during the lifting phase, and the wide grip allowed a lighter six-repetition load (77.3 kg against 80.3 and 80.0 kg).[6]

Where this leaves you: the studies are small, they use young trained men, they measure short-term EMG rather than muscle growth, and they define widths differently. They do not agree on every muscle. None of them shows that a wide pull-up grip builds more lat.

Does grip width change your reps or range of motion?

In 14 trained men, a self-selected grip averaged about 20 percent wider than a shoulder-bone-width grip. Maximal strength, repetitions to failure and the power-force-velocity profile were similar for both.[7] The test used trained men, so it says little about beginners.

For range of motion, the rotating-handle study measured mean elbow motion of 93.4 degrees in the pull-up, 100.6 in the chin-up and 99.8 on rotating handles, with spreads of about 12 to 15 degrees, so the groups overlap.[3] We did not find a pull-up study that measured how far the bar travels at different widths. The idea that wide grips shorten the range is plausible but unmeasured in the sources we checked.

Which grip is gentler on the shoulder?

A kinematics study of 11 regular pull-up trainees compared three techniques: overhand at about shoulder width, overhand with the hands on the sloped outer part of the bar (wide), and underhand at about shoulder width. The wide grip reduced the range of shoulder-blade protraction and retraction to about 10 degrees, against 22 for the overhand and 17 for the underhand grip. The underhand grip started from marked external rotation with the upper arm well away from the plane of the shoulder blade. The authors linked the wide and underhand patterns with higher sub-acromial impingement risk, and noted that high arm elevation narrows the space under the acromion.[8] In the 2026 study, the wide position raised the estimated shoulder moment by 7 percent, and the authors advise caution.[5]

Both studies measure movement and load, not injuries, and the first used a small convenience sample. Neutral grips were not part of it, and we found no study that follows people with different grips to see who gets hurt. A reasonable reading: the shoulder-width overhand grip is the best-studied option, parallel handles are worth trying if your bar has them, and very wide grips are an occasional variation.

Which grip for your first pull-up?

Pick the grip you can control and that leaves the shoulder comfortable. For most people that is hands just outside the shoulders, overhand or neutral. If you cannot get started, the underhand grip is worth a try. The chin-up showed higher biceps and chest activation,[3] which may explain why many people find it easier, though that study did not test beginners. Start each underhand rep from an active hang with the shoulder blades down, because the kinematics study flagged the relaxed underhand start.[8] Our first pull-up guide gives the progression.

How do you rotate grips for variety?

This is a practical suggestion, not a tested protocol.

  1. Choose a base grip, shoulder-width overhand or neutral, and use it for most of your sets.
  2. Add a second grip for one session a week or for the last set or two.
  3. Change one thing at a time. Switch width or orientation, not both.
  4. Expect fewer reps on a new grip the first time. Log which grip you used.
  5. Keep wide grips short and occasional. Stop the set at the first pinch in the shoulder.
  6. Swap the base grip every few weeks so no single position carries all the load.

What cues work for each grip?

  • Hand width. Film yourself from the front. At the top your forearms should be close to vertical.
  • Thumbs. Wrap them around the bar for a secure hold.
  • Shoulder blades first. Pull them down and slightly back before the elbows bend.
  • Elbows. Drive them down toward your ribs, a little in front of your body.
  • Chest to the bar. Lift the chest, not the chin.
  • Underhand. Begin from an active hang so the shoulder does not sag into the bottom position.

Which grip mistakes slow you down?

  • Going wide because it looks harder, without checking how the shoulder feels.
  • Changing grips every set, so you cannot tell what is working.
  • Starting underhand reps from a fully relaxed hang, the position the kinematics study flagged.
  • Pushing through a pinch instead of changing the grip or shortening the range.
  • Judging a grip by the pump in your back rather than by smooth, repeatable reps.

How does grip choice fit into your pull-up training?

Treat grip as a small lever. Volume, frequency and rest decide most of your progress, and the pull-up workout plan covers that structure. Use grip changes to keep the shoulders and elbows comfortable and the training interesting. A simple example: with two sessions a week, use shoulder-width overhand in the first and neutral or underhand in the second, aiming for a similar total number of reps. Again, this is a practical suggestion, not a tested program.

Frequently asked questions

Is a wide grip better for the lats?

We found no pull-up study showing that it is. In the lat pull-down, lat activation was similar across grips one to two times the shoulder-bone distance, and a 2026 pull-up study did not report a lat advantage for the wide position.

How wide should your hands be on a pull-up?

Start with hands just outside the shoulders. Trained men in one study got similar strength and reps with a self-selected grip about 20 percent wider than shoulder-bone width, so small differences do not matter much.

Is a neutral grip easier on the shoulders?

Many people find it comfortable, but the one kinematics study we found did not test it, and none compares injuries across grips. Wide and underhand patterns were flagged for impingement risk, so a neutral or overhand grip near shoulder width is a sensible choice.

Which grip is easiest for a first pull-up?

The underhand grip often is, and the chin-up showed higher biceps and chest activation than the pull-up. Start with whichever grip lets you control the movement without a pinch.

Should you switch grips often?

Switching occasionally can keep training varied and spread the load. Change one variable at a time, log it, and keep a base grip for most sets.
