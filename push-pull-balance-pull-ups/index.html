Push-pull balance: how much should you pull compared with push?

What the evidence says about ratios, posture and shoulders·8 min read

Train both pushing and pulling, and lean slightly toward pulling if you do a lot of chest work. That is a sensible habit, but no study we found gives a push-to-pull ratio that prevents injury, and the link between posture and shoulder pain is weaker than gym lore suggests. Below: what the research shows, what each exercise trains, and a sample week.

Do you need to balance pull-ups and push-ups?

In a loose sense, yes. The shoulder is worked by muscles on every side, and training only one direction leaves gaps. But there is no magic number, and the evidence that a specific balance protects you is thin.

A review of shoulder injuries in resistance training found that up to 36 percent of documented training injuries occurred at the shoulder complex. The authors listed joint and muscle imbalances as an intrinsic risk factor and poor technique as an extrinsic one. Most of the research was retrospective, mainly surveys and descriptive reports, and few studies had identified what actually predicts injury.[1] So imbalance is a plausible risk, not a measured one, and the review does not say what the balance should be.

Is there a correct push-pull ratio?

No study we found sets a push-to-pull target that prevents injury. The number also depends on how you measure it. In 33 professional firefighters, researchers calculated a strength ratio (relative bench press max divided by pull-up repetitions) and an endurance ratio (push-up repetitions divided by pull-up repetitions). The two were not associated with each other. The strength ratio tracked the bench press max (r = 0.80). The endurance ratio fell as pull-up repetitions rose (r = -0.62) and rose with body fat percentage (r = 0.40). The authors concluded the two ratios should not be used interchangeably.[2]

Two takeaways. A "ratio" can mean different things, and that study described fitness profiles, not who got hurt. Rules such as "pull twice as much as you push" are coaching conventions. We found no trial that tested them against shoulder outcomes. Treat them as a starting point and adjust by how your shoulders feel.

Can pull-ups fix rounded shoulders and posture?

The popular story: pressing shortens the front of the shoulder, pulling strengthens the back, and together they fix a rounded posture and prevent pain. Parts of it have some support. Other parts do not.

On pain, a systematic review of 10 studies looked at thoracic kyphosis, the forward curve of the upper back. It found moderate-level evidence of no difference in kyphosis between people with and without shoulder pain, and concluded that kyphosis may not be an important contributor to shoulder pain. It also found strong evidence that maximum shoulder movement is greater in an upright posture than a slouched one, though that rests on single-session studies of unclear long-term relevance.[3] The review is about the upper-back curve, not rounded shoulders as such.

On changing posture, a 2024 meta-analysis of 22 studies in people with upper crossed syndrome (forward head, rounded shoulders, hyperkyphosis) found that therapeutic exercise improved rounded-shoulder measures. The authors noted high heterogeneity, signs of publication bias and little long-term follow-up. The programs mixed strengthening, stretching and shoulder exercises, so the review cannot tell you that pull-ups, or pulling alone, do the job.[4]

A fair reading: posture and pain are not tightly linked in the data we found, and exercise can change posture measurements in the short term. Do not expect pull-ups to fix your posture, and do not assume a rounded posture is a problem if your shoulders feel fine.

What do pull-ups and push-ups each train?

In an EMG study of 25 adults, the pull-up produced average activation of 117 to 130 percent of a maximal voluntary contraction in the latissimus dorsi, 78 to 96 in the biceps, 71 to 79 in the infraspinatus, 45 to 56 in the lower trapezius and 44 to 57 in the pectoralis major.[5] So the pull-up is not purely a back exercise: the chest muscle works too.

A scoping review of 30 studies on push-up variations found the triceps and pectoralis major most active in four of six variations, with the serratus anterior highest in the push-up plus and the incline push-up. The studies used healthy young adults, so the results may not carry over to people with shoulder problems.[6]

Are pull-ups enough, or do you need rows too?

Rows are a cheap addition. In 26 adults (13 men, 13 women) doing four inverted-row variations, the biceps, lats, lower trapezius and posterior deltoid exceeded 61 percent of maximum in every variation, and the middle trapezius, upper trapezius and lumbar multifidus reached 41 to 60 percent.[7] That study did not measure pull-ups, so it cannot say rows are better. It shows that rows load several back and rear-shoulder muscles at levels used for strengthening. Our inverted rows guide covers setup and progression.

There is also a shoulder-position argument. In 11 regular pull-up trainees, the wide and underhand patterns showed shoulder kinematics that the authors linked with higher impingement risk, and they noted that high arm elevation narrows the space under the acromion.[8] That study measured movement, not injuries. Our practical inference, not a tested claim: mixing horizontal rows with pull-ups keeps part of your pulling volume out of overhead positions.

What does a balanced week look like?

This is a practical example, not a tested program. It uses about 17 pulling sets and 10 pushing sets a week, a pull-leaning split. Stop most sets one or two reps before failure.

If you cannot do pull-ups yet, replace them with negatives or band-assisted reps from our first pull-up guide. For frequency and recovery, see how often to do pull-ups, and for a pull-up-first structure the pull-up workout plan.

On volume: a meta-regression of 15 studies found that each additional weekly set was associated with about 0.37 percent more gain in muscle size, a graded dose-response.[9] That is hypertrophy data, not balance data, but it is a reason not to cut your sets for one direction just to even things out. If you want more pulling, add sets rather than remove pushing.

How do you tell if your balance is off?

These are practical signals, not diagnostic tests.

  • Lopsided progress. Your push-up or bench numbers climb for months while pull-up reps stall.
  • Pain in the front of the shoulder during or after pressing, while pulling feels fine.
  • Pinching overhead in the hang or at the bottom of a wide or underhand pull-up, a position the kinematics study flagged.[8]
  • One side lagging. Film a set from behind and compare the two sides.

If pain lasts more than a couple of weeks, wakes you at night, or comes with weakness or numbness, see a clinician rather than adjusting your split. Our pull-up warm-up and scapular muscles guide cover the shoulder-blade side.

Which mistakes should you avoid?

  • Chasing an exact ratio when the research does not support one.
  • Cutting pushing to near zero out of fear of rounded shoulders.
  • Counting only pull-ups as pulling and never training horizontal rows.
  • Pushing through shoulder pinching instead of changing the grip or range.
  • Changing the whole program at once, so you cannot tell what helped.

Frequently asked questions

What is the ideal push-pull ratio?

We found no study that sets an ideal ratio. A study of 33 firefighters showed that strength-based and endurance-based push-pull ratios were not associated, so even the definition varies. A pull-leaning split is a common coaching convention, not a proven target.

Is a pull-up a push or a pull exercise?

It is a vertical pull. The lats and biceps are most active, though the pectoralis major also works at roughly 44 to 57 percent of maximum in the EMG study we cite.

Do push-ups cause rounded shoulders?

We found no study showing that. A systematic review found moderate evidence of no difference in upper-back curvature between people with and without shoulder pain. Posture changes with exercise in short studies, but long-term effects are unclear.

Should I pull more than I push?

Many coaches recommend it, and the example week above uses about 17 pulling sets to 10 pushing sets. It is a practical default, not a tested rule. Adjust by how your shoulders feel.

Can I skip push-ups if I do pull-ups?

You can, but pull-ups do not train the triceps much and the serratus anterior is most active in push-up variations. No study we found says skipping push-ups causes injury, so it comes down to your goals.
