Pull-up variations: 11 types and who each one suits

A map of the grips, ranges and loads, with what the research does and does not show·8 min read

Pull-up variations change one of four things: where your hands go, how your palms face, how far you travel, or how much load you carry. Most of them train the same back and arm muscles, so the best one is usually the one you can do cleanly and keep doing. This page sorts eleven variations by what they change and who they suit, and says plainly where the evidence stops.

What makes a pull-up a different pull-up?

Every variation on this page changes one lever. Width moves your hands further apart or closer together. Palm direction turns your forearm over, under or sideways. Range changes how high you pull. Asymmetry gives one side more of the work. Load adds weight to the body. Once you see the lever, the names stop being a menu and become a tool kit.

The standard pull-up is the baseline: overhand grip, hands about shoulder width, chin over the bar, arms straight at the bottom. If you cannot do one yet, start with the first pull-up guide and the negative pull-up before you shop for variations.

Which grip variations are there?

VariationWhat changesWho it suits
Standard pull-upOverhand grip, about shoulder widthAnyone who can do a strict rep; the reference for everything below
Wide pull-upHands clearly outside shoulder widthExperienced lifters who want variety and have healthy shoulders
Close-grip pull-upHands inside shoulder width, overhand or parallelPeople with narrow or tight shoulders who find wide grips awkward
Neutral-grip pull-upPalms face each other on parallel handlesAnyone with irritable shoulders, elbows or wrists
Chin-upUnderhand grip, palms toward youBeginners and anyone working toward a first rep

The wide pull-up is the one people choose for a bigger back, and it is also the one with the most caveats. A 2026 study of 21 men varied grip width and hand angle. A wide hand position at a pronounced angle raised pectoralis major and rectus abdominis activation by 14 and 15 percentage points of maximal contraction, and the wide position increased the shoulder net joint moment by 7%.[3] An observational study of 11 people found that wide pull-ups showed scapula and shoulder positions linked with a higher risk of subacromial impingement.[4] Our grip width guide goes through the numbers.

The close-grip pull-up keeps the elbows closer to the body and the shoulders less abducted. That is the whole appeal. We found no EMG study that isolates it as a separate variation, so what it does to specific muscles is not established.

The neutral-grip pull-up uses parallel handles or rings. In a study of 19 strength-trained men, activation across eight arm and shoulder muscles was similar in underhand, overhand, neutral and rope pull-ups. The one difference over a full rep was the middle trapezius, which was more active overhand than neutral (peak 60.1 versus 37.1 percent of maximum).[2] If your elbows or shoulders object to an overhand bar, neutral is the first swap to try.

The chin-up is the underhand version. In a 25-person EMG study the lats were the most active muscle in the pull-up, the chin-up and a rotating-handle version, at 117 to 130 percent of maximum. The biceps and pectoralis major worked harder in the chin-up, and the lower trapezius worked harder in the pull-up.[1] Many people can do more reps with palms facing them, which is why it is the usual route to a first rep. The chin-up vs pull-up comparison has the detail.

Which range and skill variations are there?

VariationWhat changesWho it suits
Chest-to-barYou pull until the bar touches your chest or lower ribsLifters who own 10+ clean reps and want more range
L-sit pull-upLegs held straight out in front throughoutPeople who want a harder trunk demand and have the hip flexor and hamstring length
Commando pull-upHands one behind the other on the bar, body side-onIntermediate lifters who like asymmetric work and a head-past-the-bar finish
Typewriter pull-upPull up, then slide side to side with the chin at bar heightAdvanced lifters building toward archer and one-arm work
Archer pull-upOne arm pulls while the other stays straight along the barAdvanced lifters who can do many strict reps and want a unilateral step

The chest-to-bar pull-up is a longer version of the standard rep. It asks for more pulling height and tighter control at the top, and a rushed one turns into a kip. If kipping is your concern, read kipping vs strict pull-ups first, and note that chest-to-bar is also a stepping stone toward the muscle-up.

The L-sit pull-up keeps your legs straight and level with the floor. The body does not get lighter, so the trunk and hip flexors take on an extra isometric job. If your hamstrings are tight, bend the knees and treat it as a tuck pull-up.

The commando pull-up uses a staggered grip, with one hand in front of the other and the head moving to alternate sides of the bar. It is a small step toward unilateral work, and it feels different on each side, so alternate which side leads.

The typewriter pull-up and the archer pull-up both shift load to one arm at a time. In the typewriter you pull to the top and shuffle left and right with your chin above the bar. In the archer one arm pulls and the other stays long as a guide, so the effort is shared unevenly. Both demand elbow and shoulder tolerance, and both belong after you can do many strict reps.

We found no EMG or training study on commando, typewriter, archer, L-sit or chest-to-bar pull-ups as separate variations. Descriptions of what they emphasise come from coaching practice, not trials.

Which load variations are there?

The weighted pull-up adds a belt, vest or dumbbell. It is how a strong lifter keeps the rep range useful once bodyweight reps climb past ten or twelve. The ACSM position stand on progression models describes loading that increases gradually as the lifter adapts, and weighted pull-ups are the direct way to do that.[5] The weighted pull-up guide covers belts, starting loads and elbow care.

The one-arm pull-up is the extreme end. Your body weight sits on a single arm, so the load per arm doubles. Almost nobody needs it, and getting there takes months of archer, typewriter and weighted work. We found no research on how long that takes or how to progress it safely, so treat any timeline you read as one coach's experience.

What does the research not show?

The evidence on pull-up variations is thinner than the internet suggests. The EMG studies cited here used 11 to 25 participants, mostly young men, and measured muscle activity in a single session. Higher activation in one rep is not the same as more growth or more strength after months of training. No trial we found shows that one pull-up variation builds more muscle or strength than another.[1][2][3]

We also found no study on commando, typewriter, archer, L-sit, chest-to-bar or one-arm pull-ups. What those variations emphasise comes from how coaches describe them. The injury data is thinner still: the one kinematic study on grips involved 11 people and measured joint positions, not injuries.[4] Use the table as a set of reasonable starting points, then let your own joints, recovery and rep quality decide.

How do you choose a variation?

  1. Cannot do one strict rep yet. Chin-up grip, negatives, bands or inverted rows. Skip the rest.
  2. One to five clean reps. Alternate chin-up and neutral or standard grips. Keep sets short and stop before form breaks.
  3. Five to ten clean reps. Add a standard overhand pull-up, a close-grip set and an occasional wide set if your shoulders like it.
  4. Ten or more clean reps. Add weight, a longer range with chest-to-bar, or an L-sit pull-up.
  5. Strong and pain-free. Try commando, typewriter or archer sets as a small, separate block.

Rotating grips every few weeks spreads the load across your elbows and shoulders, and it keeps training interesting. It does not need a complex scheme. Pick one main variation for the heavy work and one or two supporting ones, log reps and effort, and change only one lever at a time. Sharp or pinching pain at the shoulder or elbow is a reason to switch variation or stop, not to push on.

Frequently asked questions

What are the main types of pull-ups?

Standard (overhand, shoulder width), wide, close-grip, neutral-grip and chin-up are the grip variations. Commando, L-sit, typewriter, archer and chest-to-bar change range or symmetry, and weighted and one-arm pull-ups change the load.

Which pull-up variation builds the most back muscle?

No one knows. EMG studies show small differences in which muscles work harder in a single rep, but no trial we found shows that one variation builds more muscle over time. Pick the one you can do with clean form.

Are chin-ups easier than pull-ups?

Often, yes. Underhand grip brings the biceps and pectoralis major in harder, which helps many people complete more reps. It is a common route to a first strict rep.

Is a wide grip pull-up bad for your shoulders?

Not necessarily, but it deserves caution. One kinematic study of 11 people linked wide grips to positions with higher impingement risk, and another found a 7% higher shoulder joint moment. If your shoulders feel pinched, use neutral or shoulder-width grips.

When should I try archer or one-arm pull-ups?

After you can do many strict reps, handle added weight and feel no shoulder or elbow pain. Both are advanced progressions with little published research, so go slowly.
