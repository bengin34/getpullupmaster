Latissimus dorsi and pull-ups: an anatomy lesson for people who train

Origin, insertion, nerve, what the lat does rep by rep, and what the research measured·8 min read

The latissimus dorsi is the large, flat back muscle that pulls your upper arm down and back, which is exactly the movement a pull-up demands. It works hardest in the middle of the pull, and grip changes shift its activation less than most people think. Here is the anatomy, what the research measured, and how to train it when you cannot do a rep yet.

Where is the latissimus dorsi and what does it attach to?

Start with the map. The latissimus dorsi, "the lats" for short, is the widest muscle of the back. It covers most of the lower half of your back on each side and tapers into a flat tendon that runs into the armpit.[1]

FeatureLatissimus dorsi
OriginSpinous processes of the lower six thoracic vertebrae, the thoracolumbar fascia (reaching the lumbar and sacral spinous processes), the posterior iliac crest, the lower three to four ribs and the inferior angle of the scapula
InsertionFloor of the intertubercular (bicipital) groove of the humerus, as a flat tendon that wraps around the front of teres major
InnervationThoracodorsal nerve, a branch of the posterior cord of the brachial plexus (C6–C8, mostly C7)
Blood supplyThoracodorsal artery (a continuation of the subscapular artery), plus perforating branches of the lower posterior intercostal and upper lumbar arteries
Primary actionsShoulder adduction, extension and internal rotation; pulls the trunk up toward fixed arms, as in climbing; also helps depress the shoulder girdle and assists forced expiration such as coughing

Notice the shape of that list: the muscle starts low and wide and ends high and narrow on the front of the upper arm. When it shortens it drags the arm down and back toward the pelvis; with your hands fixed on a bar, the same contraction drags your trunk up toward your hands.[1]

To feel it on your own body, stand tall and press the heel of one hand into your side, just behind the armpit. Now push the opposite elbow down and back as if squashing something under your arm. The slab that hardens under your fingers is the lat. The outer edge you can pinch below the armpit is the same muscle, the part that gives a trained back its V shape.

What does the lat do in each phase of a pull-up?

The lat's job changes along the way.

  • Dead hang. Arms straight overhead, shoulders by your ears. The lat is at its longest, stretched and fairly quiet, mainly stabilising the shoulder.
  • Initiation. Before the elbows bend, the shoulder blades drop and the shoulders pull away from the ears. This scapular depression is the lat's first visible contribution, with help from the lower trapezius. EMG timing data suggest the lower trapezius and pectoralis major start the pull and the lat and biceps finish it.[2]
  • Mid-pull. From roughly 45 degrees of elbow bend to chin height, the upper arm swings down and back: adduction plus extension, the lat's main actions, and where it works hardest.
  • Top. With the chin over the bar the lat is close to fully shortened and its leverage is poor, so the last few centimetres feel slow and the biceps and teres major take more load.
  • Lowering. The lat lengthens under tension. This eccentric phase is where you control speed and protect the shoulder, and the easiest phase to train before you can pull up.

The middle feels strongest because of the length-tension relationship: a muscle produces its most force at a mid-range length and less when very stretched (dead hang) or very short (top). StatPearls notes that lat extension and adduction are strongest when the motion starts from partial flexion or abduction, the position you pass through mid-pull.[1]

What does the research say about lat activation?

Surface EMG studies give an honest summary: the lat is the most active muscle in every pull-up variant, and grip changes move the needle less than gym folklore claims.

  • Pull-up vs chin-up. Youdas and colleagues measured 25 trained adults. Average lat activation was 117–130% of a maximal isometric reference in both exercises, the highest of all muscles recorded, ahead of biceps brachii (78–96%). The chin-up produced significantly more biceps and pectoralis major activity, the pull-up more lower trapezius; the lat did not differ meaningfully.[2]
  • Grip orientation. Dickie and colleagues compared pronated, supinated, neutral and rope grips in 19 strength-trained men. Over a full repetition, activation of the shoulder, arm and forearm muscles was similar across hand positions; the clearest difference was more middle trapezius activity with the pronated grip than the neutral grip. Concentric phases activated brachioradialis, biceps and pectoralis major more than eccentric phases.[3]
  • Grip width in the lat pulldown. Andersen and colleagues tested narrow, medium and wide pronated grips (1, 1.5 and 2 times shoulder width) at a six-repetition maximum in 15 men. Lat activation was similar across widths; narrow and medium grips allowed slightly heavier loads than wide (about 80 kg vs 77 kg), and in the lowering phase the wide grip edged out the narrow one for lat and infraspinatus. The authors concluded a medium grip may have minor advantages but 1–2 times shoulder width works.[4]
  • Pronated vs supinated in the pulldown. Lusk and colleagues had 12 men pull at 70% of 1RM. A pronated grip produced more lat activity than a supinated grip; grip width made no difference.[5]

Put together: an overhand grip at roughly shoulder width or a little wider is a sensible default for the lat, and a chin-up still trains it hard while adding more biceps and chest. These are small samples of trained men measured with surface electrodes, so treat the differences as modest tendencies rather than rules. For the practical trade-offs, see chin-up vs pull-up.

Why don't you feel your lats, and how do you fix it?

"I only feel it in my arms" is the most common complaint from new trainees, and it has a mechanical reason. If you start the pull by bending the elbows, the biceps take the load first and the shoulder blades stay shrugged. The lat never reaches its strong mid-range position with the shoulder blade set, so it contributes less and you feel it less.

Four cues fix most of it:

  1. Depress first. From the hang, pull the shoulders down away from the ears before the elbows bend, and keep them there.
  2. Elbows to the back pockets. Drive the elbows down and back toward your hips instead of pulling the bar to your chin. That turns the movement into shoulder extension and adduction, the lat's job.
  3. Chest tall. Lift the sternum slightly toward the bar; a collapsed chest puts the lat in a poor line of pull.
  4. Slow eccentric. Lower for three to five seconds. The long, loaded stretch is the easiest moment to notice the lat.

Short drills that teach the feeling: scapular pull-ups (three sets of 5–8), straight-arm pulldowns with a band before pulling work (two sets of 12–15), and pulldowns with a two-second pause at the chest while squeezing the elbows to the ribs. Arm-dominant pulling is also one of the classic pull-up mistakes, so check your form video against that list.

Which exercises build the lat before your first pull-up?

Pick two or three of these and progress them weekly:

  • Dead hang: grip and the stretched position; work toward 30–45 seconds (see dead hang benefits).
  • Scapular pull-ups: the initiation phase in isolation; add them to every session.
  • Negatives: jump to the top, lower in 3–5 seconds; three to five singles with full rest.
  • Band-assisted pull-ups: full range with the lightest band that allows 5–8 strict reps (see band-assisted pull-ups).
  • Australian rows: horizontal pulling for the lat and mid-back, scaled by body angle.
  • Lat pulldown: the closest machine substitute, pronated grip at about shoulder width.[4][5]
  • Straight-arm pulldown: isolates shoulder extension and reinforces the elbows-down cue.

The full progression from hang to first strict rep is in how to do your first pull-up.

Lat problems and injury notes: what should you watch for?

Lat strain or tear. Injuries to the latissimus dorsi and teres major are rare and appear disproportionately in the throwing shoulder of baseball pitchers; the literature consists mainly of case reports and small series. Tendon injuries are sometimes managed surgically, muscle-belly injuries usually with rest, anti-inflammatories and physiotherapy.[6] In recreational pull-up training a true tear is unusual. Sudden sharp pain in the back of the armpit during a maximal rep, with bruising or a visible change in the muscle's shape, is a reason to stop and get examined.

Shoulder impingement when shrugging. If you pull with the shoulders hitched toward the ears, the upper arm sits high in the socket and the space under the acromion narrows, often felt as a pinch at the front or top of the shoulder near the top of the rep. The fix is the same as the lat cue: depress the shoulder blades first and keep them down.

Posture and the thoracic spine. The lat attaches to the spine and pelvis through the thoracolumbar fascia. A short or stiff lat can limit overhead reach and alter trunk posture, and StatPearls flags it as a muscle to assess in people with low back pain or restricted shoulder flexion and lateral rotation.[1] Balance pulling with overhead mobility and thoracic extension work.

When to see a clinician. Numbness or tingling in the arm, weakness that does not match effort, pain that lasts more than two weeks despite easing off, pain at night, or any injury with a pop, bruising or deformity. A physiotherapist or sports physician can examine the shoulder; a web page cannot.

How often should you train your lats?

For most people, two to three pulling sessions a week, 48 hours apart, with 8–15 hard sets of vertical and horizontal pulling in total, is a reasonable range. Recovery, sleep and how the shoulders feel decide where in that range you sit. PullUp Master adjusts that number instead of fixing it: each session's load and exercises are set from how the previous one went, so a hard week lowers volume and an easy week adds it. Frequently asked questions

Do pull-ups build wide lats?

Yes. In EMG studies the lat is the most active muscle in a pull-up, and back width comes from growing the lat's outer fibres below the armpit. Enough volume and progressive load build the V shape, though your frame sets the ceiling.

Lats vs biceps: which does more work in a pull-up?

The lat. Average lat activation measured 117–130% of a maximal reference against 78–96% for the biceps. A chin-up shifts a little more work to the biceps, but the lat still leads.

Why do I feel pull-ups in my arms and not my back?

Usually because you start the pull by bending the elbows with the shoulders shrugged. Depress the shoulder blades first, drive the elbows down and back, and slow the lowering; the lat shows up once it works in its strong mid-range.

Is the lat pulldown as good as the pull-up for the lats?

It trains the same movement and is useful when you cannot do a rep yet. The pull-up adds trunk control and a heavier load; the pulldown lets you adjust weight finely. Use the pulldown as a bridge, not a replacement.

Do strong lats help posture?

Indirectly. The lat pulls the shoulders down and back and stabilises the trunk, but a short, stiff lat can limit overhead reach and tip the trunk forward. Pair pulling with overhead mobility and upper-back extension work.
