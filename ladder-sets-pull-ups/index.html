Ladder sets for pull-ups: how a 1-2-3-4 rep ladder works

Climb one rep per set, bank clean reps first, finish on your hardest rung·7 min read

A ladder set is a short run of sets where the reps climb by one each time: 1, 2, 3, 4. You rest between rungs, so ten pull-ups are spread over four easy-to-hard sets and the hardest rep count comes last, when you are warm and the pattern is groove. This guide explains why that works, how Pull Up Master builds and progresses your ladder, and how to run one well.

What is a ladder set?

A ladder is a series of short sets in which the rep count goes up by one each time. A four-rung ladder is one rep, rest, two reps, rest, three reps, rest, four reps. Every rung is a normal set with its own rest, so the whole ladder adds up to 10 pull-ups across four sets.

It differs from a straight-set plan such as 4×4 or 5×5, where every set asks for the same number. In a straight-set plan the first set is the freshest and the last is the hardest, even though the target is identical. A ladder writes that gradient into the plan: the light work comes first on purpose, and the hard work sits at the top of the ladder.

Why climb instead of repeating the same number?

There are three practical reasons and one honest caveat.

  • Clean reps while you are fresh. One or two reps is far below what you can do, so each one is slow and strict. You rehearse the pattern (dead hang start, chin clearly over the bar, controlled lowering) before fatigue shows up.
  • Less fatigue per set. You reach your hardest rung only after several smaller ones, so the work is spread across the session instead of being loaded onto one maximal set.
  • A built-in target. The top rung is one set to beat. You see progress every session without testing a max.

Volume matters for progress. A meta-analysis of weekly set volume found a dose-response relationship between the number of hard sets and muscle growth.[3] The American College of Sports Medicine position stand describes gradual progression of load and volume as the core principle of resistance training.[1] A ladder is one way to spend that volume. We found no trial that compared ladders with straight sets for pull-up reps, so treat the format as a sensible coaching convention rather than a proven winner.

How does Pull Up Master build your ladder?

The ladder lives in the Volume block, the plan the app builds when your current max is between four and ten strict pull-ups. In the app it is called "Ladder set (1-2-3-4)" and the plan line reads 4 sets × 1-2-3-4.

The top rung is your target for the day, and every rung below it steps down by one. Each level the engine moves you up adds one rep to every rung, and the ladder always keeps four rungs:

How does the app decide when to move you up?

After the last rung it compares what you logged with the top rung. If you beat it by two or more reps, the ladder goes up a level next time. If you finish below it on two sessions in a row, it goes down one. Anything in between stays put.

That makes the last rung the one that steers your plan. The early rungs are volume and practice. If the top rung feels easy, keep going and log the real number, because that number is what moves you up.

How to run a ladder set, step by step

  1. Warm up first. Do the shoulder and scapular prep from our warm-up guide. Rung one is easy, but cold shoulders are the usual reason a ladder goes wrong.
  2. Rung one: one strict rep. Start from a dead hang, pull until your chin is clearly over the bar, and lower for two to three seconds.
  3. Rest, then climb. Take the full timer. Resting short on the easy rungs is tempting and defeats the point.
  4. Keep every rung the same quality. Rung four should look like rung one. If the last reps slow down a lot, that is the hard part, not a reason to kip.
  5. Log what happened. Honest numbers keep the level right. A rung you could not finish is information, not a failure.

How long should you rest between rungs?

Longer than most people expect on the hard rungs. The ACSM position stand recommends longer rests, around two to three minutes, for heavy multi-joint lifts.[1] In a trial of resistance-trained men, a group resting three minutes between sets gained more strength and some muscle thickness than a group resting one minute.[2] That trial used weights, not pull-ups, so read it as direction rather than a prescription.

The app uses one rest time for the whole session, 90 seconds by default. You can change it in Settings. If you keep missing the top rung, try two minutes before you change anything else.

The two are not rivals. The Volume block mixes them: the ladder is one exercise in the session, and straight sets such as the 5×5 pull-up work sit next to it. If pain swaps the ladder for straight sets, the plan still works, just with a flat rhythm.

Who benefits most from a ladder?

Ladders suit people whose max sits between four and ten strict reps, which is why the app puts them in the Volume block. At that level a flat 4×4 can be close to maximal on every set, and form slips by the third set. A ladder collects the same top number with lighter sets leading up to it. It also works on days you feel flat: the early rungs act as extra warm-up, and by rung two or three you can tell whether the top rung is realistic. If you are stronger than that, higher-rep blocks use your time better. If you are weaker, negatives and bands come first.

Common ladder mistakes

  • Racing the early rungs. The point of rungs one and two is slow, strict reps. Rushing them throws away the practice.
  • Adding reps to the small rungs. Doing three on rung one turns the ladder into straight sets and spends your energy too early.
  • Kipping the top rung. A swinging fourth rep logs a number that is not your strength, and the app will level you up for it.
  • Skipping the rest. Cutting rests shrinks the volume you can do and blurs the signal the app reads.

When should you move on from ladders?

You do not have to decide. The engine retests you at the end of each block, and when your max passes ten pull-ups it moves you to the Endurance block, which is built for higher reps. If your reps stall before then, our plateau guide covers the usual causes. If you are still working toward a first strict rep, start with the first pull-up guide and come back to ladders once you can do four.

Frequently asked questions

What is a ladder set in pull-ups?

A run of short sets where the reps climb by one each time, such as 1, 2, 3, 4, with a rest between rungs. It trains clean reps first and your hardest count last.

Is a ladder better than 4×4 straight sets?

Not proven. We found no trial comparing the two for pull-up reps. A ladder gives you less total volume (10 reps against 16) but lighter early sets, which suits practising form and days you feel flat.

How long should I rest between ladder rungs?

Use the full timer, which is 90 seconds by default in Pull Up Master. Heavier efforts benefit from longer rests, so move to two minutes if you keep missing the top rung.

What happens if I cannot finish the top rung?

Log the real number. One short session changes nothing. Two in a row drops the ladder by one level, for example from 1-2-3-4 to 1-1-2-3.

Can I choose not to do ladders in the app?

Ladders are part of the Volume block, so there is no on/off switch. If you report pain the app can swap the exercise for straight sets until you are pain-free.
