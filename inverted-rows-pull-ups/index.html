Inverted rows and pull-ups: muscles, setup and how to progress

Horizontal pulling as a stepping stone to your first pull-up·8 min read

An inverted row is a horizontal pull: you hang under a bar with your feet on the floor and pull your chest to the bar, so you lift only part of your bodyweight. It trains the lats, middle and lower trapezius, rhomboids, rear deltoids and elbow flexors that a pull-up also needs, and you can make it harder in small steps. Here is the anatomy, what the EMG research shows, how to set the exercise up and scale it, and how to fit it around your pull-up practice.

What is an inverted row, and why use it for pull-ups?

An inverted row is also called a bodyweight row, an Australian pull-up or a horizontal pull-up. You lie under a bar, hold it with straight arms, keep your body in one line and pull your chest up to the bar. Your feet stay on the floor, so the arms lift only a share of your bodyweight. A pull-up asks them to lift all of it. The row trains similar muscles in a position you can adjust.

One caveat comes first. We did not find a controlled trial showing that inverted rows raise pull-up performance. The case rests on the overlap in muscles and on small difficulty steps. Treat the row as a sensible stepping stone and extra pulling volume, not a proven shortcut.

Which muscles does an inverted row work?

In the row your shoulder blades pull toward the spine, the upper arms move behind the trunk and the elbows bend. These muscles share the job.

The trunk works too and holds the plank line for the whole set. In the inverted-row EMG study the lumbar multifidus reached high activation and the rectus abdominis moderate activation.[5]

What does the research say about inverted rows?

Two EMG studies looked at the exercise directly. Youdas and colleagues recorded nine muscles in 13 men and 13 women during four inverted-row variations with bodyweight as the only load. The biceps, latissimus dorsi, lower trapezius and posterior deltoid reached very high activation (above 61 percent of a maximal voluntary contraction) in all four. The upper trapezius, middle trapezius and lumbar multifidus reached high activation (41 to 60 percent).[5] Bearing weight on one leg instead of two made no significant difference in any muscle.

Snarr and Esco compared a row on a fixed bar with a row on suspension handles in 15 people. Lat, middle trapezius and posterior deltoid activity did not differ significantly. Biceps activity was higher on the fixed bar.[6] The bar sat at about hip height, with knees bent to 90 degrees and feet flat. The authors call the exercise a beginning progression toward the bodyweight pull-up and note that straight legs or feet on a bench would raise the demand, which they say needs further study.[6]

Keep the limits in mind. The samples are small, and surface EMG shows how active a muscle is, not how much strength you will gain.

How does horizontal pulling differ from vertical pulling?

In a pull-up your arms start overhead and you pull your whole body upward. In a row your body is closer to horizontal and you pull toward your chest, so the elbows travel behind the trunk and the shoulder blades retract.

The overlap is real. In a surface-EMG study of pull-ups and chin-ups, the latissimus dorsi averaged 117 to 130 percent of its reference contraction, the biceps 78 to 96 percent and the lower trapezius 45 to 56 percent. The rep appeared to start with the lower trapezius and pectoralis major and to finish with the biceps and lats.[7] That study did not record the middle trapezius, and the two studies used different reference contractions, so do not compare their percentages one to one.

The row trains the pulling muscles and the shoulder-blade pattern with your feet on the ground. It does not train the overhead start position or the full-bodyweight grip demand, so add some vertical work.

How do you set up an inverted row?

  1. Pick a secure bar. Use a rack or Smith machine bar, a sturdy low bar, or rings. Check that it cannot slide or tip. Set it at about hip height to start, as in the study.[6]
  2. Lie under it and grip. Take an overhand grip a little wider than your shoulders.
  3. Set your legs. Easiest is knees bent with the feet flat. The standard version has straight legs and only the heels on the floor.
  4. Build the plank. Squeeze your glutes, brace your trunk and keep the body in one line from head to heels with the arms straight.
  5. Pull. Draw the chest to the bar or handles, pause briefly, then lower slowly until the arms are straight.[6]

How do you make it easier or harder?

The more horizontal your body is, the more of your weight the arms must pull.

Change one lever at a time. The EMG studies tested feet on the floor, not elevated feet, so the feet-on-bench level rests on mechanics and the authors' comment, not on measured data.[5][6] Choose a level where you can do clean reps to the bar.

Which form cues keep the row clean?

  • One line. Ears, shoulders, hips and heels stay level. Glutes tight, ribs down.
  • Shoulder blades first. Draw them back and down, then bend the elbows. This gives the trapezius the lead.
  • Elbows back. Drive them toward your ribs or slightly out, whichever your shoulders prefer.
  • Chest to the bar. Lead with the sternum, not the chin.
  • Slow down. Pause at the top, lower for two to three seconds and finish each rep with straight arms.

Which mistakes hold you back?

  • Sagging or piking hips. Piking shortens the lever and the row gets easier. Sagging loads the low back.
  • Shrugging and craning. Shoulders near the ears and a forward head hand the work to the upper traps.
  • Half reps. Stopping short of the bar or never straightening the arms skips the hardest ranges.
  • Jerking. Bouncing off your heels or throwing the hips up hides weak spots.

How do you progress from inverted rows to a first pull-up?

We found no validated threshold for the switch, so use the logic of the ACSM position stand. For novices it recommends loads that correspond to about 8 to 12 repetitions and advises raising the load once you can beat the target by one or two reps.[8] Pick the row level where a set of 8 to 12 is hard and move up when you can beat that range with clean form.

  1. Rows with knees bent and the bar at hip height.
  2. Rows with straight legs and a lower bar.
  3. Rows with the feet on a low bench and a pause at the top.
  4. Start vertical practice alongside steps 2 and 3. Use dead hangs, slow lowering from the top and the band-assisted pull-up.
  5. Test a strict pull-up once a week, fresh.

How do you program rows with pull-up practice?

The ACSM position stand recommends two to three training days per week for novices.[8] Leave a rest day between sessions.

  • Pull-up practice first. Do hangs, assisted reps or slow lowering while you are fresh. A typical starting point is a few sets of one to three quality reps.
  • Rows second. Do two to four sets of 8 to 12 rows at the current level as your main volume.
  • Progress one lever at a time. Add a rep first, then lower the bar or move the feet. Drop back a level if form breaks.
  • Respect pain. A sharp pinch in the shoulder or elbow means you stop. Pain that stays needs a clinician.

Frequently asked questions

Are inverted rows easier than pull-ups?

Yes. Your feet stay on the floor, so the arms lift only part of your bodyweight, and you can lower the bar or raise the feet to scale the load. That makes the row a practical starting point.

Can inverted rows replace pull-ups?

Not completely. Rows train many of the same muscles in a horizontal direction, but they do not train the overhead start position. We found no controlled trial showing that rows alone raise pull-up numbers, so add some vertical pulling.

Which muscles do inverted rows work?

Rows work the lats, the middle and lower trapezius, the rhomboids, the posterior deltoid and the elbow flexors, with the trunk holding the plank line. EMG testing found very high activation in the lats, lower trapezius, posterior deltoid and biceps.

How high should the bar be?

Start around hip height, which is the setup used in the EMG study. Adjust so you can pull your chest to the bar for clean reps. A lower bar makes the body flatter and the row harder.

Should I use an overhand or underhand grip?

Both train the same muscles. Grip changed lat activity in one comparison of the EMG study, but both grips activated the main pulling muscles strongly. Use the grip that feels comfortable on your shoulders and elbows.

When can I move on to pull-ups?

There is no tested cut-off. A practical rule is to progress the row once you can beat a set of about 8 to 12 clean reps, and to start hangs and assisted pull-ups well before that.
