Pull-up isometric holds: top, mid and low positions

What the research says about joint angle and how to program the hold·8 min read

An isometric pull-up hold means you stop at one position and keep your muscles working: chin over the bar, elbows near a right angle, or arms almost straight. Lab studies find that the strength you gain is largest around the angle you train, so use more than one position and pair holds with full-range work. No trial has tested holds for a first pull-up, so the dosing below is a cautious coaching framework.

What is a pull-up isometric hold?

An isometric hold is a pull-up with the movement removed. You get to a position, stay there and keep your muscles working while the bar and your body stay still. The best-known version is the top hold, also called a flexed arm hang: chin over the bar, elbows bent, held for time. You can also stop halfway with your elbows near a right angle, or low with your arms slightly bent.

A hang with straight arms is a different exercise, the dead hang. We cover it in our dead hang guide, so this page deals with holds where the elbows are bent.

Holds are useful when you cannot pull yourself up yet. You still practise the grip, the shoulder-blade position and the elbow bend, and you spend real time in positions that a normal rep passes through quickly.

Which muscles hold you up, and why does elbow angle matter?

The elbow flexors do much of the holding. The biceps brachii flexes the forearm and, with the elbow bent, is the strongest supinator of the forearm.[1] The brachialis lies underneath it and is described as a pure flexor of the elbow that works in every forearm position.[2] Your lats and lower trapezius keep the shoulder blades and upper arms in place, and your grip muscles keep the bar in your hands. Our biceps and brachialis guide goes deeper on the elbow flexors.

How much force a muscle can produce depends on its length, which at the elbow means the joint angle. A study of isometric elbow flexion tested 18 young men, 15 young women and 11 male bodybuilders at seven elbow angles, where 180 degrees is a straight arm. Peak strength came at 120 degrees in the men and bodybuilders and at 90 degrees in the women. At 75 degrees, the most bent angle tested, torque was 20 percent lower than at 120 degrees in the men and 25 percent lower in the bodybuilders, with no difference between the two angles in the women. The authors suggested that larger muscles may explain the drop at the shortest lengths.[3]

That was a seated test and not a pull-up hold, and the top of a pull-up is usually more bent than 75 degrees. Read it as a hint and not as a measurement: a hold at a different elbow angle is a different job for the same muscles.

Does isometric training only work at the angle you train?

Largely yes, with a twist. The authors of a 9-week trial describe isometric training as producing large but highly angle-specific adaptations. In it, 33 men trained the quadriceps of one leg isometrically at four joint angles and the other leg with conventional dynamic lifting. Isokinetic strength rose about the same in both legs (10.5 percent isometric, 10.7 percent dynamic), while isometric strength rose more in the isometrically trained leg (18.0 percent against 13.1 percent).[4] Spreading holds over several angles seems to have kept the carry-over to moving strength intact.

A second study split 16 men into two groups who trained knee extension isometrically three times a week for 6 weeks, one at a short muscle length and one at a long muscle length. The short-length group showed a clear angle-specific force gain of 13.4 percent around the trained angle, linked to nerve-drive changes. The long-length group did not show that pattern, and their muscle volume and cross-sectional area grew instead.[5]

Both studies used the thigh muscles in a lab. They show how isometric adaptation works in general, not what happens in a pull-up.

Which hold angle carries over best to full pull-ups?

A systematic review of 26 research outputs found that isometric training at longer muscle lengths produced more muscle growth than equal volumes at shorter lengths, and that long-length training carried over better to dynamic performance. It also found large gains in muscle size and maximal force at any intensity, with high-intensity contractions (70 percent of maximum or more) needed to improve tendon structure and function. The authors point out that few studies met their criteria.[6]

A narrative review adds that isometric work causes less fatigue than dynamic training and gives strength that is specific to the trained angle. It suggests holds can strengthen a weak joint position, and that for maximal strength you should use multiple angles or one targeted angle.[7] For you, the practical reading is to avoid training only the top hold. A mid or low hold puts the muscles at a longer length and may transfer better to the first part of a rep.

One trial looked at pull-ups directly. Thirty advanced to elite climbers trained twice a week for 5 weeks with hangboard pull-up work in an isometric, eccentric or plyometric format, alongside a control group. Maximum strength improved in all three training groups (by 2.2 to 5.0 percent), but speed variables improved only in the eccentric and plyometric groups, and range of motion only in the eccentric group.[8] With 6 to 8 people per group and elite participants, it says little about beginners, and the abstract does not describe the hold positions. The isometric work built strength but showed no gain in speed or range.

How do you do the top, mid and low holds?

  1. Get into position. Use a box or a jump so you start at the position you want. An underhand grip is often easier for the top hold.
  2. Set your shoulders. Pull your shoulder blades down so you are not hanging from your neck. Keep your ribs down and your legs slightly in front of you.
  3. Hold and keep breathing. For the top hold your chin stays over the bar. For the mid hold, stop with your elbows near 90 degrees. For the low hold, stay just above a full hang.
  4. End the set when form breaks. That means your chin dips below the bar, your shoulders creep up to your ears or you start to swing.
  5. Lower under control. Turn the end of the hold into a slow negative and reset on the box. Do not drop straight into a hang.

If you cannot reach the top yet, do the mid or low hold, or use a band to reach the top.

How long and how often should you hold?

No pull-up trial gives a hold time. Laboratory guidance, from a narrative review, uses contractions of 1 to 5 seconds at 80 to 100 percent of maximum for strength, with 30 to 90 seconds of total contraction time per session across multiple angles. For muscle growth it suggests 70 to 75 percent of maximum held for 3 to 30 seconds, with more than 80 to 150 seconds per session over more than 36 sessions.[7] Those percentages refer to a measured maximum that you cannot measure at a bar. A bodyweight hold you can only keep for a few seconds is close to your maximum, so use time as a stand-in.

Can isometric holds help with sore tendons?

Possibly, but not for the pull-up. In a small crossover study, six volleyball players with patellar tendon pain did one session of isometric contractions. Their pain during a single-leg decline squat fell from 7.0 to 0.17 out of 10, against a fall from 6.33 to 3.75 after isotonic contractions, and the relief lasted at least 45 minutes.[9] That is one joint, one session and six people. We found no study of holds for elbow or shoulder pain in pull-ups. If your elbow hurts, read our elbow tendon guide, and stop a hold if pain sharpens.

What are the limits of the evidence?

  • Most isometric studies use the thigh muscles in a lab, not a bodyweight pull-up.
  • The only pull-up trial involved 30 climbers over 5 weeks.
  • No study compares holds with negatives or band reps for a first pull-up.
  • Hold times in the table are a starting point, not a prescription.

Frequently asked questions

How long should you hold the top of a pull-up?

No trial gives a number. A cautious start is 3 to 4 sets of 5 to 10 seconds, adding time only when every set ends with your chin over the bar and your shoulders down. Lab guidance for maximal strength uses short contractions of 1 to 5 seconds, so very long holds are not required.

Are isometric holds better than negatives for a first pull-up?

We found no study that compares them in beginners. In climbers, isometric, eccentric and plyometric pull-up training all raised maximum strength, but only the eccentric and plyometric groups improved speed. Using both is a reasonable approach.

Which elbow angle should you train?

More than one. Isometric gains are largest around the trained angle, and reviews favor longer muscle lengths for muscle growth and carry-over, so add a mid or low hold to the top hold.

Is a flexed arm hang the same as the top hold?

Yes. Both mean holding with your chin above the bar and your elbows bent, for as long as you can keep good form.

Can isometric holds help with elbow or shoulder pain?

We found no pull-up study on this. One small knee study showed short-term pain relief after isometric contractions, which is not proof for the elbow. If pain sharpens, stop and see our elbow tendon guide.
